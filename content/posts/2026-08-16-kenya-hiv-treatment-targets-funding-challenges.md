## The Illusion of a Health Milestone

The government is celebrating a milestone that is already evaporating. While the Ministry of Health parades the 95-95-95 treatment targets as a triumph of domestic policy, these statistics mask a crumbling financial architecture that relies on money that is no longer coming.

The script is familiar: claim a victory, credit the local administration, and ignore the benefactor in the room. Kenya has met the global treatment goals for HIV, and new infections have plummeted by 80 percent since 2010. Yet, this success is a borrowed suit. Behind the polished data points lies a structural insolvency, as international health funding saw a staggering contraction from 8.8 billion dollars in 2024 to 7.3 billion dollars in 2025. This 1.5 billion dollar global shortfall is not a distant fiscal headline; it is a death sentence for the local supply chain if the state continues to treat donor dependency as a permanent line item.

### The Math of a Policy Failure

Public health experts and economists are not cheering. They are counting the incoming bills that the Treasury has no plan to cover. For years, the PEPFAR and Global Fund grants acted as a buffer, allowing the government to treat HIV programs as external responsibilities rather than core domestic obligations. Now that the global well is drying up, the lack of a contingency fund becomes a glaring indictment of long-term planning.

Transitioning from donor-funded programs to state-led financing requires more than just rhetoric. It requires a fundamental shift in the budget, yet health allocations remain stunted by the same fiscal pressures seen elsewhere, such as the desperate push to [fix trade deficits](https://zandani.co.ke/article/kenya-eyes-new-china-zero-tariff-policy-to-cut-trade-deficit) through external policy shifts. When the grant money officially hits the floor, the responsibility for procurement, laboratory logistics, and clinical staffing will fall squarely on a Kenyan exchequer that is already strained. The government cannot claim credit for the 95-95-95 achievement while maintaining a budget that treats the HIV response as a perpetual charity case.

### Geography of the Coming Crisis

The risk is not distributed equally. In counties like Homa Bay, Kisumu, and Migori, the reliance on donor-funded outreach is absolute. These regions have become the primary battlegrounds for viral suppression, but they are also the most vulnerable to a sudden withdrawal of support. If the funding gap is not bridged by domestic tax revenue, the clinics that kept the infection rates down will be the first to shutter.

The strategy of importing medical success while exporting the financial risk is reaching its terminal phase. While officials focus on the glory of the 80 percent infection reduction, they offer nothing to the patients in Nyanza who depend on a system that is fundamentally insecure. Even as some leaders pivot their attention to shifting political allegiances-such as the recent exit of high-profile figures from the [Jubilee Party](https://zandani.co.ke/article/pauline-njoroge-resigns-from-jubilee-party)-the silent reality remains that the drugs currently sustaining 1.2 million Kenyans are paid for by parties that are systematically walking away.

The Ministry of Health has successfully hit a target, but it has failed the sustainability test. When the global funding well runs dry, who exactly will pay to keep these facilities open?
