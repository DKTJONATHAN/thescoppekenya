## Paying for Leaks: The Hidden Penalty in New Water Bills

Small business owners are branding the latest move by the Water Services Regulatory Board as a double-edged sword, arguing that residents are being forced to bankroll the administrative incompetence of their local water providers through steep, performance-linked tariff spikes.

The Water Services Regulatory Board recently released a gazette notice confirming new water and sewerage tariffs for six counties, covering the period from mid-July 2026 to July 2027. These rates are tied directly to company performance indicators, meaning that utilities failing to curb Non-Revenue Water, or water lost to leaks and illegal connections, are passing the cost of those inefficiencies directly onto consumers. While regions with better infrastructure may see measured adjustments, areas plagued by systemic rot are facing the sharpest hikes, effectively charging households for water that never even reaches their taps.

### Voices from the Taps: The Real Cost of Inefficiency

In Nyahururu, where the water infrastructure has long been criticized for its aging, leaky network, local cafe owner Maina Gichuhi says the new pricing feels like a punishment for living in a neglected zone. He points out that his monthly overheads are already strained, and this hike forces him to pay for water that evaporates through cracked pipes miles before it reaches his shop. He notes that the logic of performance-based pricing breaks down when the consumer has no control over the utility's crumbling pipework.

Over in Kitui, residents are echoing a similar sentiment of frustration regarding the fairness of these metrics. Sarah Mwende, who runs a small catering business, argues that the regulatory framework treats water access like a luxury rather than a basic need. She finds it absurd that her business is being billed at higher rates to meet performance targets when service delivery remains sporadic at best. For her, the policy feels like a tax on desperation, where those with the least reliable service end up subsidizing the company's inability to fix its own leaks.

In Nairobi, the conversation shifts toward the disparity between different utility zones. Financial analyst Dennis Omondi suggests that the performance-based model is technically flawed because it prioritizes corporate fiscal recovery over actual service expansion. He warns that when utilities struggle with Non-Revenue Water, often exceeding 60 percent in some jurisdictions, passing those losses to the customer creates a cycle of poverty. Instead of fixing the infrastructure, these companies are simply squeezing the public to balance their books, leaving households to choose between high utility debts or restricted access.

### Who Carries the Weight?

The core of the issue lies in the measurement of Non-Revenue Water, the industry term for water that is produced but lost before it reaches the customer. Across these six counties, the disparity is stark. Utilities in Nyeri and parts of Nakuru have historically maintained tighter control over their distribution networks, whereas other regions continue to bleed water through illegal connections and outdated, unmetered systems. By linking tariffs to these metrics, the regulator is essentially deciding that the customer should pay for the utility's inability to police its own network.

Experts familiar with the regulatory framework note that while performance targets are standard for incentivizing efficiency, they rarely account for the socioeconomic reality of the Kenyan consumer. If a utility company is failing to meet its targets, the sudden hike in price for a family consuming between 1 and 6 cubic metres of water can be the difference between buying food or settling a utility bill. The pressure to meet these targets is driving a wedge between the regulatory mandate to recover costs and the constitutional guarantee of accessible water for all.

As these new rates take hold, the tension is mounting. While the board maintains that these adjustments are necessary to sustain the operational viability of water providers, the public mood is one of deep cynicism. Recent societal frustrations-whether it is the silence on rising femicide cases or the struggle to recover from senseless attacks like the one that left gospel singer Rachel Wandeto critically injured-have left the public with little patience for bureaucratic explanations. For the average Kenyan, the rising cost of water is just one more burden in an already strained household budget.

There is a growing demand for transparency in how these targets are calculated. Citizens are asking why the penalty for a utility's technical failure always lands in the pocket of the consumer, while the management responsible for the leaks remains untouched by the economic consequences of their mismanagement. The public is watching, and for many, the latest bill is becoming a symbol of a system that prioritizes balance sheets over the thirsty.
