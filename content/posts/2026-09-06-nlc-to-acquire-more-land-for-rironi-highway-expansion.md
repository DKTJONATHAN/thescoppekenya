## Compensation Fatigue Hits Kiambu as New Land Acquisition Notices Drop NLC to acquire 13 more land parcels for Rironi highway expansion is the central subject of the update.

The latest gazette notice from the National Land Commission has been met with weary sighs rather than surprise, as residents along the James Gichuru-Rironi corridor face yet another round of uncertainty regarding their property rights and the stalled highway expansion project.

The National Land Commission has signaled its intent to acquire 13 additional land parcels to push forward the long-delayed James Gichuru-Rironi highway expansion. This move follows a turbulent six-year history where project-affected persons have remained in a state of limbo, trapped between grand government infrastructure promises and a reality of frozen property sales. While officials point to a Ksh20.4 billion budget allocated for these acquisitions, residents who have waited years for their initial payouts remain skeptical. A formal public inquiry is now scheduled for October 30 at the Kinoo Chief's Office, where the commission intends to verify claims and discuss compensation logistics for the land affected by this latest phase of the road project.

### Voices from the Dust of Kinoo

For many families in Kinoo and Uthiru, the news is less about progress and more about the endurance of their exhaustion. Samuel Mbugua, a small-scale plot owner, says his family has been unable to renovate their home since 2019 because every structure built on the land could lead to a devaluation during the inspection process. He notes that the constant threat of acquisition has effectively killed the local real estate market, leaving them with property they cannot sell, improve, or fully utilize.

A business owner in the area, Sarah Wanjiku, reflects the frustration of those who have seen neighbors pass away or lose their livelihoods while waiting for the NLC to finalize payments. She describes the situation as a slow motion erosion of security, where the promise of government compensation feels like a carrot perpetually kept out of reach. To her, the October inquiry is just another bureaucratic hurdle that forces residents to spend more time and money on lawyers rather than on reclaiming their financial stability.

Meanwhile, observers point to the role of litigators like Okiya Omtatah, who have kept the project under legal scrutiny for years. Residents often mention that these legal battles provided the only real buffer against forced evictions, even if the trade-off was a total freeze on development. There is a palpable sense that the legal tug-of-war has left the local community bearing the brunt of the costs, while the actual highway, much like the broader infrastructure discussions happening elsewhere, remains a distant prospect for daily commuters.

### Living in the Shadow of the A8

The devaluation of property in this corridor is the most visible scar of the project. Real estate agents in Uthiru report that potential buyers now avoid the area entirely, fearing that any investment will be swallowed by the road expansion or buried in years of litigation. This is a sharp contrast to the economic activity seen in other sectors, such as the logistics discussions currently surrounding the Ministry of Transport’s focus on roadside stations. While the government plans for efficient transit hubs and safer roads, the people holding the deeds to the land remain trapped in a cycle of filing, gazetting, and waiting.

The upcoming inquiry will serve as a litmus test for whether the commission can expedite the compensation process or if residents are destined for another cycle of delays. Given the history of the James Gichuru-Rironi route, expectations are incredibly low. With the government pushing for completion, the pressure on the 13 landowners is mounting, but the appetite for further cooperation has faded into a cautious, defensive silence. The road remains a work in progress, but for those living along its edges, the path forward feels like a dead end.
