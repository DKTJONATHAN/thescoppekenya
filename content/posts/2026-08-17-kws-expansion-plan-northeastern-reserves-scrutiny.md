## Beyond the Paper Parks: A Demand for Conservation Accountability KWS expansion plan for Northeastern reserves faces scrutiny is the central subject of the update.

Dr. Erustus Kanga, the proposal from the Kenya Wildlife Service to revitalize dormant reserves and secure additional protected areas across Garissa and Wajir carries a weight of history that your administration cannot afford to disregard.

Dear Dr. Kanga, 

Your expansion strategy invites the public to envision a thriving new frontier for biodiversity in Northern Kenya. However, this vision is overshadowed by the 37-year legacy of Malka Mari National Park. Established in 1989, this 876-square-kilometre site serves as a sobering reminder of state-led conservation shortcomings. For nearly four decades, it has operated merely as a paper park-a territory existing on official maps that fails to provide substantive wildlife protection or generate the economic benefits promised to neighboring communities. As you move to revive dormant sites such as Arawale and Rahole, or look to earmark community land in Wajir for state oversight, you must address why these ventures will not repeat the history of Malka Mari. Taxpayers and local residents are no longer satisfied with administrative cartography that projects authority while neglecting genuine local development.

### Adhering to the Community Land Act

The friction between KWS directives and the legal mandates of the Community Land Act has reached a breaking point. Your current approach relies on the assumption that the state retains the unilateral power to designate acreage as protected. Yet, legislative shifts have returned land authority to the people who hold legal title to these areas. 

In regions such as Boni and Mandera, residents have observed how centralized oversight frequently results in restricted access to vital water sources and grazing lands rather than ecological flourishing. By treating these landscapes as unoccupied canvases for government management, the KWS risks alienating the very stakeholders who are essential for monitoring poaching and mitigating human-wildlife conflict. 

Modern conservation in the North must move away from the outdated, colonial-era focus on exclusion and fences. Instead, it must embrace a collaborative framework that honors land tenure. Just as the nation evaluates the merits of the [Ruto Champions New African Financial Model to Reduce Aid Reliance](https://zandani.co.ke/article/ruto-champions-new-african-financial-model-to-reduce-aid-reliance), so too must we demand a more progressive and transparent economic model for our environment.

### Prioritizing Community Success Over State Stagnation

We possess substantial evidence that the path forward involves bolstering existing community conservancies rather than establishing new state-run bureaucratic centers. Locally managed projects across Northern Kenya have demonstrated a greater capacity for habitat restoration and local employment than the idle, KWS-managed reserves. 

These community-led initiatives leverage indigenous knowledge and local rangers, creating a model that is significantly more resilient than infrequent patrols dispatched from distant outposts. It is increasingly difficult to justify further budgetary allocations for new infrastructure when the institutions currently under your care, specifically the neglected reserves in Garissa, remain underfunded and functionally invisible. 

We must apply the same rigorous standards of oversight here as we do when [Ruto Orders Investigation Into Fatal Arson Attack on Rachel Wandeto](https://zandani.co.ke/article/ruto-orders-investigation-into-fatal-arson-attack-on-rachel-wandeto). If we are to hold public figures accountable for security and justice, we must apply that same scrutiny to the financial management of our national heritage. Will your budget prioritize the revival of stagnant, non-performing assets, or will it be reallocated to support the proven success of community-managed ecosystems?

Transparency is not merely a suggestion; it is a fundamental requirement for your tenure. Before you designate a single additional acre of community land as a protected reserve, you must present an audit of your agency’s past failures. Please provide a clear, public breakdown of the funds currently earmarked for these new projects compared to the capital required to remediate the broken systems already under your watch.

The people of Northeastern Kenya are looking for partners, not landlords. They demand a conservation strategy that prioritizes human dignity alongside ecological health. I look forward to seeing your response to these concerns as you navigate the future of our northern lands.
