## The Three-Hour Race: Why Students Are Struggling to Keep Pace with HELB’s New Portal Mandates HELB Issues New Guidelines for HEF Portal Activation Links is the central subject of the update.

Frustration is boiling over in campus hostels and rural cyber cafes as students grapple with a rigid three-hour expiry window for new Higher Education Loans Board portal activation links. While officials argue this adds a layer of security, the demand for instant connectivity has left those in areas with shaky internet struggling to secure their financial future.

The Higher Education Loans Board has recently issued a fresh set of operational guidelines for the HEF portal, aimed at streamlining account creation for the 2026/2027 academic cycle. The core of these new rules involves a strictly enforced three-hour lifespan for every activation link sent to an applicant's email address. Once this time elapses, the system locks the user out, forcing them to restart the entire registration process. Alongside this, the board has issued a stern warning against engaging with unofficial social media pages that promise to expedite loan processing, citing a sharp rise in phishing attempts and data theft. This digital transition aims to move away from the clunky, paper-heavy manual systems of the past, yet for many, the shift from manual queues to digital time-limits feels like trading one headache for another.

### When the Clock Outruns the Connectivity

For students like Kelvin, a third-year engineering student in a remote part of western Kenya, the new timeline feels disconnected from his reality. He spent the better part of Tuesday evening tethering his laptop to a weak mobile data signal, only for his connection to drop just as the activation email arrived. By the time the signal stabilized and he refreshed his inbox, the link had already expired. He is not alone in this digital scramble. Many students in rural regions are reporting that the time spent waiting for a stable network often exceeds the window provided by the portal. While some see the shift as a necessary evolution to ensure data integrity and prevent fraudulent account creation, others view it as an elitist barrier that rewards those with high-speed fiber internet and penalizes those relying on fluctuating cellular data.

### Voices from the Digital Frontlines

The reaction across student forums is polarized. One student from a Nairobi-based university expressed that while the portal is certainly faster than the old manual forms that could take months to process, the stress of the countdown timer is unnecessary. He argued that if the system is meant to be efficient, it should account for the fact that not everyone in the country enjoys 5G speeds or constant electricity. Another student, who recently attempted to register from a busy cyber cafe in Nakuru, noted that the pressure to verify the account within three hours often leads to mistakes, forcing even more restarts. Meanwhile, a student leader from a public institution admitted that while the cybersecurity warnings are valid-given the number of scammers preying on desperate learners-the board needs to create a more forgiving interface for those experiencing genuine technical delays.

### The Trade-off Between Security and Access

The board maintains that these measures are essential to protect sensitive personal financial information from being compromised by third-party actors. By tightening the session parameters, they hope to minimize the risk of accounts being hijacked or linked to incorrect profiles. However, the current reality highlights a stark divide: students who can access reliable, high-speed internet are successfully navigating the portal with ease, while those in underserved areas find themselves trapped in a loop of expired links and re-registration attempts. As the deadline for the 2026/2027 cycle looms, the urgency for a more adaptive system that balances security with the practical realities of Kenyan infrastructure grows louder.

The public mood remains anxious, with many waiting to see if the board will introduce a grace period for those who can prove that technical failures, rather than neglect, caused their missed windows.
