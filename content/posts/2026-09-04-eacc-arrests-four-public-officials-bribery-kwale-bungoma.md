## The Hidden Tax Killing Small Businesses

Detectives from the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission apprehended four public officials this week in Kwale and Bungoma counties. These arrests highlight a recurring cycle where minor bureaucratic hurdles are weaponized to extract cash from struggling local entrepreneurs across the country.

### Why does a twenty thousand shilling bribe cripple a business?

For a small cyber cafe in a town like Kimilili, a demand for twenty thousand shillings is not merely an inconvenience. It is often the entirety of a month's profit. When an enforcement officer forces a payment under the threat of closing a shop, that money is diverted from essential needs like electricity bills, software licenses, or even simple equipment maintenance.

If that same shop owner nets thirty thousand shillings in a good month, a twenty thousand shilling bribe represents two thirds of their take-home income. This theft strips the business of its ability to reinvest or grow. When these small enterprises fail, the local economy loses a source of employment and a vital service provider. The cumulative effect of these small extractions serves as a hidden, predatory tax that prevents informal businesses from graduating into the formal sector. While we watch the [fraying threads of power](https://zandani.co.ke/article/fraying-threads-power-kenya-kwanza-looming-rift) at the national level, these local-level shakedowns ensure that the grassroots economy remains stagnant.

### How do extortion demands change based on the industry?

The nature of the bribe often depends on the specific vulnerability of the business owner. In Diani, security officers from Kenya Power allegedly targeted electricity users, exploiting the fear of sudden disconnection. Utility-related extortion is particularly damaging because it forces business owners to choose between paying the bribe or suffering a complete shutdown of their operations. A pharmacy or a cold-storage facility, for instance, cannot afford even a single day of power loss.

Conversely, regulatory enforcement officers in markets like Kimilili often focus on administrative compliance. They might target licensing documentation, business permits, or fire safety certificates. Because these regulations are often complex and sometimes intentionally opaque, officers use the threat of immediate closure to demand instant cash. While the utility-based bribe is about maintaining the infrastructure of business, the regulatory bribe is about maintaining the right to exist in the marketplace. Both forms function as a toll booth on the road to basic economic survival.

### What is the long-term impact on regional development?

When public officials view their roles as opportunities for extraction rather than service, the primary victim is regional economic stability. Investors, even those operating at a micro-level, need a predictable environment to succeed. When the cost of doing business includes an unpredictable, illegal surcharge, entrepreneurs begin to minimize their exposure. They stop upgrading their premises, hire fewer workers, and reduce their stock levels.

This climate of fear discourages innovation. If a business owner knows that success or expansion will only draw more attention from predatory inspectors, they will intentionally stay small and invisible. Such behavior stunts the growth of regional hubs like Diani and Kimilili. These towns have the potential to become engines of local development, but that potential is stifled when every profit margin is systematically eroded by those tasked with upholding the law. Every bribe paid is a nail in the coffin of local economic growth, ensuring that wealth remains concentrated in the hands of the few rather than circulating through the community.

Every bribe paid to a public official is a direct subtraction from the growth of our local communities.
