## AGOA status under threat due to transshipment allegations

A new White House report identifies Kenya as an opportunistic hub for bypassing U.S. tariffs on Chinese-made goods, a situation that now threatens the nation's preferential trade access. This discovery places Ksh71 billion in annual exports at direct risk of sudden cancellation.

### How does shifting labels affect local factory workers?

The trade pact known as the African Growth and Opportunity Act, or AGOA, allows Kenya to send thousands of garment products to the United States duty-free. This arrangement is the primary reason why Export Processing Zones, or EPZs, have flourished across the country. These hubs employ thousands of Kenyans who manufacture everything from denim jeans to high-end apparel for American consumers.

If the United States decides that these factories are being used to hide the true origin of goods, the duty-free status could be revoked. For a worker in an EPZ, this shift would mean their employer loses a significant price advantage over competitors in other nations. Suddenly, Kenyan goods would face the same heavy tariffs as Chinese imports. This would force companies to either absorb the cost, leading to potential layoffs, or exit the Kenyan market entirely to protect their profit margins. The economic survival of thousands of families depends on maintaining a clean reputation in the eyes of American customs officials.

### Why does transshipment undermine legitimate exporters?

Transshipment is a deceptive practice where goods manufactured in one country-in this case, China-are shipped to a second country like Kenya, rebranded as locally made, and then exported to a final destination to avoid heavy taxes. While a few local logistics operators might gain short-term fees from this illegal activity, the long-term damage to the country is profound.

When Kenya is flagged as a peripheral hub for such schemes, American authorities rely on satellite data and artificial intelligence to monitor trade routes more aggressively. This scrutiny creates administrative bottlenecks that penalize honest businesses. Genuine Kenyan farmers and manufacturers, who have spent years building a legitimate export brand, find their containers delayed or subjected to exhaustive physical inspections at U.S. ports. These delays ruin perishable produce and push buyers toward more predictable markets in countries like Morocco. Honest businesses end up paying the price for the shortcuts taken by a few opportunistic middlemen.

### What is the financial scale of this trade tightrope?

The figure of Ksh71 billion represents the current volume of trade flowing through these preferential channels. Losing this access would be more than just a diplomatic embarrassment; it would trigger an immediate contraction in the manufacturing sector. Export-led industrialization has been a pillar of recent economic policy, aimed at turning Kenya into a regional production powerhouse.

The threat of sanctions forces the government to choose between tolerating small-scale smuggling and protecting the macro-economic stability of the entire industrial sector. While authorities work to improve internal oversight, the U.S. Detective Border agency is already tracking movement patterns. The shift suggests that trade policy is no longer just about diplomacy but is now tied to real-time, data-driven border enforcement. Kenyan exporters are now operating in an environment where a single mislabeled shipment can trigger a review of national eligibility. For those involved in the logistics and manufacturing chains, the pressure to ensure absolute supply chain transparency has never been higher, especially as even unrelated incidents, such as recent criminal activity involving [police impersonators](https://zandani.co.ke/article/police-impersonator-arrested-nairobi-firearm-ammo) or [planned power maintenance](https://zandani.co.ke/article/kenya-power-maintenance-outages-may-28), highlight the need for stricter institutional controls across all sectors.

Legitimate market access is only as secure as the weakest link in the supply chain.
