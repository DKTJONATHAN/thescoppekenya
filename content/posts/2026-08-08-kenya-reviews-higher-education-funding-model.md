## The False Promise of Accessible Degrees

The Kenyan government is currently scrutinizing its higher education funding framework as the reality of a 35,000-student dropout surge in 2024 forces a reckoning with how we finance the dreams of our youth.

To the Ministry of Education and the Higher Education Financing Board:

Your current funding model is not a ladder for the disadvantaged. It is a paper scaffold that is collapsing under the weight of unrealistic fiscal expectations. We are seeing a structural disconnect between the state-sponsored banding system and the financial survival of the average Kenyan student. When 35,000 students vanish from public TVET institutions, we are not witnessing a lack of ambition. We are witnessing an exodus caused by the cruel realization that these young people were ushered into debt for an education they could never afford to complete.

### Debt as a Barrier to Mobility

The banding system currently classifies students by their supposed financial capability, yet it ignores the immediate, harsh reality of daily subsistence. A student from an impoverished background, who is offered a loan to cover tuition while lacking funds for rent or food, is not being supported. They are being set up for insolvency before they even hold a certificate. We have created a system that prioritizes enrollment numbers on a spreadsheet while failing to provide the secondary support needed for retention. This is not inclusivity. This is an invitation to academic abandonment. 

We are treating education as a financial product rather than a public good. If we look at international pay-as-you-earn models, the primary focus is on ensuring that the graduate's income is high enough to trigger repayment without causing poverty. In our local context, we are instead imposing rigid, early-stage repayment pressures that discourage graduation. Graduates leave school already tethered to debts that grow while they struggle to find entry-level employment in a stagnant economy. Much like the complex legal battles surrounding assets in coastal regions, where the High Court recently rejected the ARA bid to seize Mombasa apartments, our students are finding that their legal obligations often clash with the reality of their economic survival.

### The Myth of the Future Payoff

The current strategy relies on the hope that a degree or diploma is a guaranteed path to wealth. This ignores the current job market. While industries such as infrastructure and oil refinery expansion-referenced in discussions about major investments in Lamu and Mombasa-are vital, they require specific, sustained skill sets that our current revolving-door system is failing to produce. We are churning out dropouts instead of specialists. 

The human cost is even more severe when we consider the instability surrounding our campuses. When violence and economic pressure become the defining features of student life-a reality reflected in the tragic events involving students in Nyamira-we see that the pressure of the funding model is only one part of a wider failure to protect our youth. We must stop pretending that debt is a substitute for state support. We are mortgaging the future of a generation for the sake of an administrative model that views human beings as interest-bearing loans.

If the government truly intends to reform this framework, you must abandon the rigid, debt-first approach. Shift the focus from loan disbursements to robust, grant-heavy scholarships that prioritize completion rates over enrollment metrics. Stop measuring success by how many students you have loaded with debt, and start measuring it by how many students are successfully entering the workforce without the shackles of premature insolvency. 

The generation you are currently tracking as dropouts deserves more than a revision of your funding portal. They deserve a policy that recognizes their humanity. Will you commit to a grant-led model that ensures no student leaves their studies simply because they ran out of money for their next meal?
