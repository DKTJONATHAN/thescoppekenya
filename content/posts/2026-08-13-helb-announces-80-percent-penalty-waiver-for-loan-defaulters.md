## The Endless Cycle of Student Debt Forgiveness HELB Announces 80 Percent Penalty Waiver for Loan Defaulters is the central subject of the update.

The Higher Education Loans Board has rolled out a fresh amnesty program, slashing accrued penalties by 80 percent for borrowers who clear their principal loan balances. This marks yet another attempt to thin out a portfolio of 380,000 delinquent accounts that have collectively ballooned into a staggering Sh42 billion liability, tethering a generation of graduates to a restrictive credit history.

### A History of Diminishing Returns

The narrative of student loan repayment in Kenya has long been defined by this predictable cycle of amnesty and stagnation. Since the inception of the loan recovery scheme, the board has periodically turned to penalty waivers as a blunt instrument to coax repayments from a demographic that is often functionally unable to pay.

- 2013: The first major push for waiver-backed repayment saw modest success, yet failed to curb the growth of the debt pool as unemployment rates for degree holders began their climb.
- 2018: A more aggressive outreach campaign attempted to leverage Credit Reference Bureau listing threats, which resulted in a spike in loan applications for "clearance certificates" but minimal long-term improvement in consistent monthly installments.
- 2023: The government introduced a tiered interest rate structure, yet the volume of defaulters remained static, suggesting that the problem was not one of borrower apathy, but of a broken economic pipeline.

The 80 percent amnesty structure is significantly more generous than the traditional 50 or 60 percent offers seen in previous years. However, this shift in strategy speaks to an underlying panic regarding the sheer weight of the Sh42 billion debt pile. While the board frames this as a recovery tool, it inadvertently highlights the reality that hundreds of thousands of Kenyans remain trapped in a cycle where they are technically employed yet remain unable to meet basic credit obligations.

### The Structural Trap of the Default Label

Labeling 380,000 individuals as defaulters obscures a deeper, more systemic failure within the Kenyan job market. Many graduates spend years in the informal sector or in precarious internships where income is neither sufficient nor consistent enough to service a loan. When a borrower is listed with a Credit Reference Bureau, their path to entrepreneurship or formal employment is effectively blocked. This creates a self-reinforcing trap: the debt prevents the borrower from gaining the financial standing required to ever actually pay back the loan.

Financial analysts argue that the psychological impact of being blacklisted is profound. Young professionals often bypass formal financial institutions entirely, opting for unregulated digital lenders to avoid the scrutiny of their student loan history. This fragmentation of credit behavior suggests that the amnesty program, while helpful for those with existing liquid assets, does little to address the 120,000 borrowers who struggle even with smaller installments, such as the minimum Sh5,000 mark.

### Outlook for the Debt Pool

History suggests that these amnesty windows act as temporary stopgaps rather than permanent solutions. When the current waiver expires, the board will likely return to the same friction points that existed prior to this announcement. The debt pool grows because the economy fails to provide the entry-level stability required to facilitate repayment. Without a fundamental shift in how the state bridges the gap between graduation and stable income, the board will find itself launching another iteration of this campaign in the near future.

The system repeatedly mistakes an inability to pay for a refusal to pay.
