## Beyond the Thread: How Kenyan Small-Scale Exporters View the 2028 AGOA Lifeline

While the government celebrates the extension of the African Growth and Opportunity Act until 2028, the mood on the ground among local entrepreneurs is less about victory and more about the crushing reality of infrastructure costs.

The U.S. government has officially signed a two-year extension of the trade program, ensuring Kenyan manufacturers keep duty-free access to American markets through December 31, 2028. This authorization, part of the wider Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act of 2027, arrives just as industries were preparing for a potential cliff edge. Officials like Lee Kinyanjui are now pushing for an urgent shift toward diversifying exports, aiming to move the national portfolio beyond Za Ndani textile quotas that have dominated the trade relationship since the agreement first launched in 2000.

### The View from the Factory Floor

In the Export Processing Zones, the atmosphere remains tense despite the policy extension. For the 66,000 workers currently tied to the textile sector, the news provides a sense of job security, but many feel the benefits are trapped at the top of the supply chain. Workers note that while the U.S. market stays open, the daily grind of meeting massive production quotas for global fashion labels leaves little room for wage growth. The reality for these employees is a repetitive cycle of shifts where the macro-economic win of an extended deal feels far removed from the struggle to meet rising living costs in Nairobi.

Independent SME owners are voicing a different kind of frustration. They argue that AGOA is largely a playground for large-scale industrial firms that already possess the logistics networks to navigate U.S. customs. A small-scale exporter of leather goods explained that he has been trying to access these markets for three years, but the combination of high electricity costs and the lack of specialized cold-chain logistics makes competing with factory giants impossible. To him, the extension is a lifeline for the well-established, while the smaller, innovative manufacturers remain sidelined by local infrastructure barriers.

### Strategic Shifts and the Skepticism of Growth

Government representatives are attempting to pivot the national narrative toward diversification. The plan is to push agricultural goods and artisanal products into the American sphere, hoping to broaden the base of beneficiaries. However, analysts point out that international market standards for these goods are significantly more stringent than those for bulk textiles. The local skepticism stems from a history of broken promises regarding technical support. Many manufacturers feel that without direct subsidies or lower credit rates, the promise of exporting a wider variety of goods will remain a theoretical goal rather than a practical achievement.

The conversation in the city often wanders toward other pressing headlines, from the latest [DCI moves to link student criminal records to certificates of good conduct](https://zandani.co.ke/article/dci-to-link-student-criminal-records-to-good-conduct-certificates) to the buzz surrounding sports, such as [George Onyango winning the CBC Super Welterweight Title](https://zandani.co.ke/article/george-onyango-wins-cbc-super-welterweight-title-nairobi). Yet, for the industrial sector, the AGOA extension is the only story that matters. The next two years are now a race against time. If the government fails to fix the energy and transport bottlenecks that keep SME costs high, the 2028 expiration date will likely return to haunt the economy as a looming crisis rather than a distant deadline.

The public mood remains one of cautious observation, waiting to see if this diplomatic win translates into a single extra shilling for the small producer or if the status quo simply holds.
