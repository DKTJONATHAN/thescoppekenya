## Electoral Commission Faces Scrutiny Over Omission of Results Livestreaming IEBC Clarifies Election Law Amendments Amid Transparency Concerns is the central subject of the update.

The Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) has formally addressed the Election Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, confirming that the proposed legislation does not include provisions for the real-time public livestreaming of election results. While the commission maintains that manual voter identification remains prohibited, the absence of a mandate for digital transparency has sparked debate regarding the future of electoral oversight.

In its statement, the IEBC contends that the 2026 Bill is designed to harmonize the Election Act with the 2018 High Court ruling in the case of Katiba Institute versus the Attorney General. The commission maintains that its objective is to streamline administrative functions by repealing redundant sections of the law. Despite this focus on legal compliance, observers note that the legislation overlooks the digital standards established during recent electoral cycles. Since 2017, the shift toward real-time digital visibility has functioned as a central pillar for maintaining public confidence in the integrity of the polling process.

### The Conflict Between Digital Transparency and Verification Protocols

A primary concern centers on the procedures established for validating Form 34A documents. The IEBC has clarified that verification will involve a comparative analysis between transmitted digital images and physical records delivered to national tallying centers. While the commission presents this as a safeguard against clerical errors, critics argue that the reliance on physical document verification introduces systemic latency. Because digital images can suffer from transmission quality issues, the emphasis on physical verification creates a bottleneck that may hinder the speed of results reporting.

By omitting a guarantee for a real-time public portal, the commission has left the door open for a shift toward centralized bureaucratic oversight. If digital images are categorized as secondary to physical forms, the verification process may revert to a system where data is inaccessible to the public until the final stages of the count. This creates a disconnect from the expectations set during the 2022 elections, where voters relied on instant access to polling station returns as a means of tracking the count in real time.

### Legislative Language and the Implications for 2027

The 2026 Bill utilizes highly technical language to outline its proposed adjustments, a move that some legal analysts suggest obscures the risks of administrative backsliding. By excluding a livestreaming requirement, the IEBC creates a regulatory vacuum that could allow for restricted data access in future cycles. This struggle for institutional accountability echoes broader corporate governance concerns, such as the recent efforts by shareholders to challenge board oversight in major firms. In both instances, the underlying issue remains a tension between those who manage internal processes and the stakeholders demanding external verification.

The commission’s stance suggests a prioritized focus on the physical security of ballots over the immediate information rights of the voting public. This tension between administrative efficiency and transparency has become a defining characteristic of the preparations for the 2027 general elections. There is growing concern that the digital infrastructure previously heralded as a tool for progress may be sidelined in favor of more traditional, closed-door tallying methods.

Public scrutiny is expected to intensify as the Bill moves into the parliamentary committee stage. These upcoming hearings provide a forum for stakeholders to demand that the IEBC explicitly codify the livestreaming of results. Whether lawmakers will mandate these transparency measures or support the commission's preference for physical verification remains a pivotal question. The final decision rests on the supplementary regulations that the IEBC is scheduled to release in the fourth quarter of this year. As the electoral roadmap for 2027 takes shape, the commission must balance its commitment to judicial compliance with the public’s clear demand for digital accountability.
