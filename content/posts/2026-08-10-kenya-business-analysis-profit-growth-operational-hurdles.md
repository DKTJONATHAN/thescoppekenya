## Financial Records Amid Structural Friction

EABL and NCBA recently reported a combined net profit surpassing KSh 30 billion. While these figures indicate strong corporate performance, they simultaneously highlight a widening disconnect between the profitability of major blue-chip entities and the stagnation of Kenya's industrial sector. Despite robust balance sheets, the broader real economy is contending with a five-month decline in production and ongoing administrative obstacles.

### The Profit Paradox in Corporate Results

East African Breweries Limited (EABL) posted a net profit of KSh 18.23 billion for the 2026 financial year, accompanied by a dividend of 12.70 KSh per share. In the banking sector, NCBA Group announced a H1 2026 profit of KSh 12.39 billion, representing a significant 49.4 percent year-on-year increase. These results demonstrate how large enterprises utilize pricing power and digital infrastructure to shield their margins from macroeconomic headwinds.

Conversely, the Stanbic Bank Kenya Purchasing Managers Index (PMI) highlights a more difficult reality for the private sector. Industrial output has now contracted for five consecutive months. While the July 2026 PMI data showed a brief surge in hiring, this trend likely reflects a necessity to manage labor compliance rather than an expansion of operational capacity. Businesses are currently forced to absorb rising operational costs, and their ability to maintain profitability is largely dependent on the capacity to pass these costs to the consumer. For the investor, these earnings reflect the market dominance of specific firms rather than a broad-based economic recovery.

### Judicial Bottlenecks as a Hidden Corporate Tax

The long-term viability of this corporate growth is increasingly threatened by administrative and legal inefficiencies. According to Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission (EACC) reports for May and June 2026, only four of 43 cases referred to the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions (ODPP) proceeded to trial. This low rate of litigation and oversight serves as a functional tax on the private sector, forcing firms to allocate capital toward navigating bureaucracy rather than toward innovation or capacity expansion.

Legal and judicial inertia significantly elevates the risk premium for domestic capital projects. When the resolution of commercial disputes is delayed or transparency is lacking, institutional investors become reluctant to commit to long-term ventures. Banks like NCBA have managed to grow through retail credit expansion, but this model relies on a stable consumption environment. If the current lack of a predictable legal framework persists, the reliance on high-margin credit products may become untenable, leaving domestic firms vulnerable to market volatility.

### Divergent Paths for Capital Allocation

Facing limited growth opportunities at home, local pension funds are increasingly exploring international investments. The Dangote Refinery is set to launch a US$ 5 billion IPO this October, and projections suggest regional pension funds may allocate up to US$ 500 million to the offering. This shift underscores a strategic preference for markets that offer greater regulatory clarity and tangible infrastructure development compared to the supply-side constraints prevalent in the Kenyan manufacturing sector.

Meanwhile, the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) continues to manage liquidity through its interest rate policy, with the Monetary Policy Committee expected to hold the Central Bank Rate at 8.75 percent on August 11. While this move aims to provide currency stability, it offers minimal relief for manufacturers struggling with high input costs. The profit expansion seen in the beverage and banking sectors stands in stark contrast to the sustained slide in industrial output. 

Market participants are now turning their attention toward forthcoming regulatory filings and upcoming banking sector audits. These documents will be critical in determining whether current profit margins are truly sustainable or if they are merely temporary anomalies in a cooling economy. Investors must weigh the efficiency of dominant firms against the systemic risks posed by the ongoing production decline and the high cost of doing business within an environment marked by persistent regulatory friction.
