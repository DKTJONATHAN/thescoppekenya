## The Illusion of Digital Security

Banks peddle a myth of iron-clad digital safety while actively building doors that open for the first person to hold a stolen ID card. The Milimani Small Claims Court just shredded this facade, ruling that financial institutions cannot simply point at 'customer negligence' to escape the bill for their own rapid-activation protocols.

The court's decision to order a bank to pay Ksh511,000-plus interest-after fraudsters wiped out a victim's account is a systemic indictment. It signals the end of the impunity banks have enjoyed by prioritizing onboarding speed over verification integrity. While the banking sector aggressively markets mobile banking apps as frictionless, they have intentionally sidelined robust identity checks to ensure faster customer acquisition. This shift from physical branches to digital-first onboarding has created a dangerous vacuum. Fraudsters know that while an official branch manager might scrutinize a signature or facial features, an automated digital interface is programmed to prioritize completion rates over safety.

### The Profitability of Lax Protocols

The banking industry operates on a calculated risk model. By stripping away friction in the activation process, they increase their user base and transaction volumes, which translates directly into higher fees and profitability. If a security failure occurs, Za Ndani playbook is to blame the consumer for losing their phone or identification. This defense has been the bedrock of the banking sector’s strategy for years, allowing them to ignore the reality that their systems were essentially designed to be exploited by anyone with a basic set of stolen credentials.

By pushing for 'express' account setup, banks have essentially gamified the KYC process, turning it into a box-ticking exercise rather than a security gate. When a customer is robbed, the speed with which a criminal can hijack a digital identity is not a sign of user-error. It is a sign of a digital architecture that values the transaction over the identity of the person making it. If a bank can turn a profit from the convenience of its app, it must also bear the cost of the vulnerabilities it built into the system to achieve that convenience.

### The Need for a Kill-Switch

The legal shift away from blaming the victim represents a critical turning point. Courts are finally recognizing that a consumer cannot be held responsible for the inadequacy of a bank's backend verification. A standardized, mandatory biometric kill-switch for digital profiles is long overdue. This would allow a customer to instantly freeze their entire digital ecosystem the moment a loss occurs.

Currently, the onus remains on the victim to navigate a labyrinthine and often unresponsive customer support process while their money is siphoned away in real-time. Banks seem more interested in protecting their margins than providing a genuine emergency off-ramp for victims of identity theft. As we see in other sectors where accountability is demanded, such as the ongoing scrutiny over technology in sports, the lack of trust in automated systems is becoming a defining feature of the era. Whether it is the debate over VAR in football, as seen when [Guardiola admits never trusting VAR after controversial Arsenal goal ruling](https://zandani.co.ke/article/guardiola-admits-never-trusting-var-after-controversial-arsenal-goal), or the life-altering consequences of digital banking fraud, the common denominator is a growing public awareness that systems are often rigged to serve the institution, not the individual.

The era of banks treating consumer funds as collateral damage for their rapid expansion is facing a overdue reckoning. If digital banking is as secure as the advertisements claim, why are these institutions still fighting tooth and nail to avoid responsibility when their walls crumble? Is the industry waiting for a full-scale loss of public confidence before they stop designing systems that make it easier for a thief to steal than for a victim to recover?
