## The Milk Cartel’s Convenient Border Crossings

Cabinet Secretary Lee Kinyanjui claims Kenya is importing Ugandan milk to fill a drought-induced deficit, yet the bureaucratic speed of these imports masks a calculated shift that prioritizes corporate distributors over the survival of the Kenyan smallholder dairy farmer.

### A Manufactured Scarcity

The narrative of climate-driven production failure is a convenient shroud. While Ministry officials point to erratic weather cycles in the Rift Valley and Central Kenya to explain the supply dip, they remain silent on the state of the domestic dairy budget. Official data indicates that funding for climate-resilient animal feed and artificial insemination subsidies has been redirected, effectively starving local cooperatives of the capital needed to maintain production during dry spells.

The reality is that while the average farmer in Nakuru struggles to keep their herd fed, the government has fast-tracked the 46th COMESA Policy Organs Meeting protocols to grease the wheels of cross-border trade. This is not just about filling a supply gap. It is about a managed transition where the lucrative processing sector shifts its loyalty to Ugandan producers. By relying on imports from regions like Ankole and Mbarara, large-scale processors bypass the logistical and financial costs of supporting small-scale Kenyan cooperatives, effectively freezing out the local competition while maintaining their own profit margins.

### The Profit Margin Chasm

Large-scale processors enjoy massive economies of scale when sourcing from Greater Masaka and Ntungamo, where production costs remain lower due to fewer regulatory overheads than those imposed on Kenyan farmers. These processors are the primary beneficiaries of the current import surge. While the consumer might see a temporary stabilization in supermarket milk prices, the long-term trade balance reveals a stark decline in domestic investment. The government has prioritized regional trade volumes to satisfy COMESA requirements, leaving the Kenyan smallholder to wither on the vine.

The budget allocation for drought-mitigation inputs-meant to shield the Kenyan sector from the exact conditions now cited as a justification for imports-has seen a consistent decline in actual disbursement. This shortfall in domestic support acts as a deliberate policy lever, forcing the market to look toward Malaba for replenishment. The reliance on regional imports creates a dependency cycle that leaves Kenyan farmers unable to bargain for better prices, as they are constantly undercut by the influx of lower-cost milk from across the border.

### Who Controls the Tap?

The mechanics of this trade shift resemble other recent patterns of state-sanctioned economic realignment, often scrutinized under the lens of patronage and influence. Just as the [George Ruto linked to anonymous biker Brayo in viral theory](https://zandani.co.ke/article/george-ruto-linked-to-anonymous-biker-brayo) discussion highlights the murkiness of public-facing narratives, the milk import policy is shielded by the complex jargon of regional protocols. Much like the broader themes discussed in [The Emperor's New Anthem: Unmasking the Pan-African Entertainment Illusion](https://zandani.co.ke/article/the-emperors-new-anthem-pan-african-entertainment-illusion), the rhetoric of pan-African trade unity is frequently deployed to silence those questioning who actually holds the domestic market share.

These trade protocols, while ostensibly designed for regional harmony, have been weaponized to protect the commercial interests of entities that act as intermediaries between the Ugandan farms and Kenyan retail shelves. These intermediaries rarely pass the savings on to the consumer in a meaningful way. Instead, they capture the spread between the low procurement cost in Uganda and the retail price in Nairobi. Meanwhile, local cooperatives that lack the political access to influence these COMESA-level agreements are left to absorb the losses, further destabilizing a sector that once served as the backbone of rural prosperity. If the government’s priority is indeed food security, why is the policy architecture designed to make domestic production more expensive and importation more profitable for a select few?
