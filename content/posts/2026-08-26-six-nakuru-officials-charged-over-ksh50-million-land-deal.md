## When Bureaucracy Fails: The Cost of Improper Land Acquisition Six Nakuru officials charged over Ksh50 million land deal is the central subject of the update.

Six public officials from the Nakuru County government recently appeared in court to answer for their alleged roles in a procurement process that bypassed standard legal channels during a Ksh50 million land purchase intended for a community health facility.

The case stems from the acquisition of the plot identified as LR Dundori/Lanet Block 5/894, a site originally earmarked for public service expansion within Umoja Lanet Ward. Instead of a straightforward transaction, the project became entangled in a web of procedural shortcuts that the prosecution argues violated both the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act and basic physical planning requirements.

### Why did LR Dundori/Lanet Block 5/894 fail the due diligence test?

At the heart of the matter is the gap between intention and execution. In theory, county governments must conduct a rigorous valuation and site assessment before committing public funds to real estate. For this specific parcel, investigators discovered that the vetting process lacked the necessary oversight from independent planners and auditors.

When county departments skip the due diligence stage, they risk purchasing land that may have unresolved encumbrances, incorrect zoning designations, or inflated market valuations. In this instance, the failure to verify the legal status and structural suitability of the land meant that the county government paid a significant premium for a site that was not ready for the construction of health infrastructure. This oversight suggests a breakdown in the internal checks meant to protect the public purse from private vendors who may not be offering fair value or clear titles.

### How do these procurement patterns affect service delivery?

The charges involving this single plot are linked to a broader set of 29 contracts that the investigation is now scrutinizing. For residents of Umoja Lanet Ward, these procurement hurdles are not just administrative paperwork errors. They represent tangible delays in the construction of clinics and community health centers. Every time a contract process is compromised by the omission of mandatory procurement steps, the timeline for service delivery shifts further into the future.

The funds tied up in these irregular deals are essentially diverted from their original purpose, which was to build facilities capable of housing equipment and medical personnel. When the cycle of planning is broken, taxpayers foot the bill for legal fees and investigations rather than for the primary healthcare services they were promised. Understanding whether your local county project follows the law involves looking for signs of transparency. Taxpayers should check if the county tender notice-such as Tender No. NCG/DP/LHPP/05/2019/2020-was publicly advertised with clear specifications for land use. If a project proceeds without a visible public valuation report or a clear planning approval from the National Land Commission, it is often a sign that procurement rules are being treated as suggestions rather than requirements.

### How can taxpayers monitor local land transactions?

Transparency in county land deals relies on the accessibility of the procurement lifecycle. Citizens can verify whether a project is legitimate by cross-referencing county budget documents against the actual physical progress on the ground. When a multi-million shilling project is announced, the county is legally obligated to maintain a public record of the tendering process.

An effective way to audit these processes is to monitor the county assembly minutes where land acquisitions are approved. If a purchase is authorized without a detailed feasibility study attached to the proposal, it warrants deeper public scrutiny. By keeping an eye on the procurement portal of the county government, residents can see if the vendors involved are qualified and if the purchase price aligns with current land rates in the area. Strengthening these oversight practices is the only way to ensure that public money goes into building essential infrastructure rather than into the hands of those who view government contracts as opportunities for personal gain.

Sound policy relies on the ability of citizens to demand proof of due diligence before the shovels hit the ground.
