## Bridging the Dental Divide: Why Kenya Must Empower Its Frontline Oral Health Workforce

The dental crisis across Kenya represents a profound structural failure that denies millions of citizens access to basic, life-altering care. While modern clinics flourish in wealthy urban enclaves, the vast majority of our population encounters a desert of oral health services. Current policies and traditional medical frameworks have failed to address this chasm, leaving our people to suffer in silence.

To the Ministry of Health and the relevant professional regulatory bodies:

The statistics paint a grim portrait of national oversight. Our current ratio stands at one dentist for every 37,000 citizens, a figure that is starkly disconnected from the World Health Organization recommendation of one per 7,000. While internal debates over professional prestige continue, 98 percent of our adult population endures preventable gum disease. Furthermore, oral health services are currently absent in 87 percent of our health facilities. This is not a matter of limited national ambition; it is a fundamental failure of strategic planning.

### The International Case for Mid-Level Care

We are currently disregarding a proven international blueprint that has flourished for over a century. New Zealand inaugurated a model in 1921 that utilized mid-level practitioners to deliver essential dental treatments, successfully reconciling the tension between clinical demand and public accessibility. Today, more than 50 nations, including Finland, Norway, and the Netherlands, rely on similar task-sharing frameworks to ensure that rural and marginalized communities are not neglected.

Our 867 licensed Community Oral Health Officers (COHOs) represent the functional equivalent of this global standard. Since the inaugural class graduated in 1988, these officers have served as the vital, overworked backbone of our rural health infrastructure. To dismiss their contributions as a regulatory obstacle or to harbor doubt about their professional credentials is to ignore the reality on the ground. In regions such as Turkana, Mandera, or Wajir, a COHO is often the singular professional preventing a community from descending into systemic dental neglect.

### Moving Beyond Urban-Centric Gatekeeping

Policy discourse in Nairobi frequently centers exclusively on the needs of top-tier dental surgeons. However, these practitioners remain heavily clustered in major urban centers, with 43 percent of the entire dental workforce operating within these capital hubs. This reality enforces a geographic inequity where access to health is dictated by one’s address. If we persist in prioritizing academic gatekeeping over the tangible well-being of patients, we are choosing to preserve a broken, elitist status quo.

The COHO provides the missing link that our system currently refuses to fully empower. By utilizing their training to deliver both preventative and restorative care, we could decentralize our services and finally unlock the doors for the 58 percent of our population who live within 20 kilometers of a facility yet find no oral care available. Similar patterns of bureaucratic stagnation exist in other sectors, where entrenched traditions and rigid control mechanisms prevent necessary progress.

### A Mandate for Strategic Integration

The path forward demands that we abandon the protectionist attitudes that have stifled our dental workforce for far too long. We must adopt a model of task-shifting grounded in robust legal recognition and an expanded, modernized scope of practice for our COHOs. This proposal is not intended to devalue the specialized expertise of dentists; rather, it is an acknowledgment that our national health crisis requires a unified, team-based approach where every qualified hand is mobilized.

We have squandered enough time bickering over titles and professional borders while the public suffers from manageable infections and chronic, avoidable pain. The necessary infrastructure is already in place, the professionals are already trained, and the international evidence is undeniable. We must stop viewing the COHO as a secondary tier of care and begin recognizing them as an essential, primary pillar of our national health system.

Will the Ministry commit to a concrete policy shift that formally integrates these officers into the primary healthcare agenda by the upcoming budget cycle? Or will we continue to watch the dental divide widen while the effective, proven tools for fixing it sit idle?
