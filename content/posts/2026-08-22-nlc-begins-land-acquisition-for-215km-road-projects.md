## The Price of Concrete Progress

The National Land Commission has officially signaled the beginning of the end for hundreds of families across the North Rift and Kajiado. While officials tout connectivity, the move to seize land for 215 kilometres of new tarmac ignores the reality of those who call these ancestral tracts home.

The state frames these road projects as vital arteries for trade, specifically linking the North Rift to broader economic hubs. However, the official gazette notices published this week mask a cold reality. Behind the sterile talk of infrastructure development and the division of the Barpello-Tot-Sigor-Marich Pass road into three distinct lots, families in Baringo, Elgeyo Marakwet, and West Pokot are facing the forced dissolution of their agrarian livelihoods. In Upper Matasia, the inquiry scheduled for September 16 at the Chief's Office is not a consultation. It is a procedural hurdle designed to clear the path for the excavators, leaving residents to wonder if the compensation packages will even cover the cost of relocating, let alone replacing their ancestral soil.

### Compensation versus Market Reality

Economic analysis suggests a glaring disparity between the market value of rural agricultural land and the rates the National Land Commission typically offers. Historical data from KeNHA projects reveals a consistent pattern where compensation payouts suffer from indefinite, systemic delays, forcing displaced families into years of financial purgatory. While the government calculates the value of the land based on rigid, bureaucratic formulae, it fails to account for the loss of sustainable food production, established grazing patterns, and the intangible cost of communal displacement.

In the North Rift corridors, where the path cuts across fertile zones, the acquisition is framed as a public good. Yet, for the smallholder farmer, the road represents the destruction of a legacy. The 48 kilometres slated for development in specific segments of this region do not serve the local economy so much as they transit through it, effectively turning vibrant, productive farms into buffer zones for freight trucks and regional transport logistics. This disconnect between the promise of national progress and the local economic crater left in its wake remains the most underreported aspect of the project.

### The Paper Trail of Displacement

The logistical breakdown of the 215 kilometres-ranging from the 43 kilometre and 46 kilometre stretches in the North Rift to the smaller 7.023 kilometre segments elsewhere-suggests a fragmented approach to acquisition. This fragmentation serves an administrative convenience, allowing the state to move through counties piecemeal while minimizing the appearance of a mass eviction. By breaking the projects into manageable lots, KeNHA avoids the scrutiny that a singular, massive displacement project would inevitably attract.

Much like the shifts in social dynamics observed elsewhere, such as how younger generations are redefining marriage and gender norms, the state is attempting to force a rigid, outdated development model onto a landscape that has long relied on local autonomy and land ownership. The families in Upper Matasia who find themselves on the wrong side of the planned survey markers are witnessing the same bureaucratic indifference that once plagued families in other infrastructure corridors. If history serves as any guide, the legal process will be efficient, the compensation will be protracted, and the community impact will be treated as a secondary casualty of a construction timeline that prioritizes speed over human sustainability.

Development is often measured in cubic metres of concrete and kilometres of asphalt, yet these metrics conveniently omit the lives ground under the wheels of the state. As the September 16 inquiry date approaches for Kajiado, the question remains: when the asphalt settles and the ribbon is cut, who is the infrastructure actually designed to serve, and at what point does the cost to the displaced outweigh the convenience of the traveler?
