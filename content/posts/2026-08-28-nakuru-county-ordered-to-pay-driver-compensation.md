## Nakuru County and the Hidden Cost of Disposable Labor Nakuru County ordered to pay driver Ksh4.1 million for injuries is the central subject of the update.

Nakuru County Government has been ordered to pay Ksh4.1 million to a former driver following a 2021 accident that resulted in the amputation of his leg. While the court ruling provides a semblance of justice, the case exposes a calculated culture of administrative negligence where public workers are left without formal employment records, creating a shadow workforce designed to evade liability.

### The Paperless Trap

The ruling handed down on August 26 highlights a disturbing trend across devolved units: the strategic failure to document the existence of casual and semi-casual staff. In the case of the driver involved in the December 2021 accident, the county essentially argued that without formal payslips, the worker’s claim to lost future earnings was speculative at best. This is not a clerical oversight. It is a defense mechanism. By keeping staff off the formal payroll, county administrations deny them the basic protections afforded by labor laws.

When a worker is crippled on the job, the lack of a paper trail serves as a shield for the employer. If there is no record of employment, there is technically no liability. This driver was lucky enough to secure a court victory, but the process reveals how difficult it is for the vulnerable to force the government to acknowledge their status as employees. Just as [Dennis Ombachi faces public backlash over his professional involvement with the government](https://zandani.co.ke/article/dennis-ombachi-faces-public-backlash-over-his-professional-involveme), there is a growing disconnect between state actions and the rights of those they utilize. The absence of documentation acts as a structural barrier to justice, forcing citizens to fight uphill legal battles against institutions that hold all the cards and all the files.

### Damages and the Price of Negligence

The Ksh4.1 million award is a significant hit to the county budget, yet it barely captures the true cost of systemic abandonment. The breakdown includes Ksh3 million for loss of earning capacity, Ksh800,000 for pain and suffering, and Ksh300,000 for medical expenses incurred at Nakuru Level 5 Hospital. The court rejected the county’s attempt to dismiss the claim based on the missing payroll records. Instead, the magistrate established a vital legal precedent: diminished earning capacity can be assessed through objective evidence of labor even when the employer refuses to provide salary records.

This ruling effectively closes a loophole that county governments have used to exploit the informal workforce. For years, officials have relied on the assumption that a worker without a signed contract is a ghost, invisible to the laws governing workplace injury. By shifting the burden of proof, the court has signaled that the state cannot hide behind its own administrative incompetence to avoid compensating those it has left permanently disabled.

While the headline figure grabs attention, the broader implication is whether devolved units will change their hiring practices. In a climate where [Kenya Property Prices Rise Driven by Demand for Standalone Homes](https://zandani.co.ke/article/kenya-property-prices-rise-driven-by-demand-for-standalone-homes), the cost of living continues to skyrocket, yet the state continues to operate on the labor of people it refuses to officially recognize. If this Ksh4.1 million penalty does not force Nakuru County to standardize its employment records, it will be clear that the state is not merely disorganized; it is indifferent to the long-term well-being of the people who keep its wheels turning.

How many more workers must be maimed while serving in the shadows before the county decides that an honest contract is cheaper than a court-ordered settlement?
