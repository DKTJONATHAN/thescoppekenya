## The Cost of Looking Away Why Funding NCD Care Is Crucial for Universal Health Coverage is the central subject of the update.

I remember sitting in a Murang'a community hall last Tuesday, watching a grandmother clutch a prescription she finally felt she could afford. For years, her hypertension had been a silent, ticking clock. Seeing the relief in her eyes was a poignant moment, but it also served as a painful nudge regarding our national priorities. We often lose billions by prioritizing emergency interventions over the quiet, steady work of prevention.

Kenya is currently navigating a major transition toward Universal Health Coverage through the Social Health Authority. Yet, as we debate premiums and administrative structures, a profound fiscal blind spot persists. We are failing to address the primary threat to our health budget: non-communicable diseases. Unless we begin to view conditions like diabetes and hypertension as urgent economic concerns rather than secondary health issues, our entire coverage model risks collapse under the weight of late-stage care.

### The Arithmetic of Neglect

The problem here is not just a clinical burden; it represents a significant macroeconomic risk. Economic projections suggest that seven major chronic illnesses could strip approximately Sh5.8 trillion from our economy in the near future. This figure is staggering, yet much of it remains avoidable if we pivot away from our current hospital-centric obsession. Currently, our healthcare system operates like an ambulance stationed at the bottom of a cliff. By the time the patient arrives, the costs to both the household and the public exchequer have ballooned beyond recovery.

The statistics are well-documented, yet policy action remains frustratingly slow. About 24 percent of Kenyan adults now struggle with hypertension. Perhaps most harrowing is the fact that 78 percent of those diagnosed receive no treatment whatsoever. This gap in care is a quiet thief of our collective productivity. Every untreated patient is a potential candidate for a stroke or cardiac event, disasters that drain far more from public intensive care units than a lifetime of affordable, consistent community monitoring would ever require.

### A Lesson from the Grassroots

Murang'a County offers a compelling case study on how we might disrupt this cycle. By empowering community health promoters to reach into the most remote villages, the county successfully screened 174,000 people for diabetes and 141,000 for hypertension. This was not the result of massive, expensive hospital construction projects. Instead, it was a disciplined, localized effort to identify health risks before they escalated into permanent disabilities. By expanding the diagnostic net to reach the people, the county effectively lowered the barrier to entry for the entire healthcare system.

If the Social Health Authority aims for long-term sustainability, this community-driven approach must become its financial backbone. Scaling these initiatives nationwide would transform our health budget from a reactive emergency fund into a proactive investment vehicle. As businesses in Kenya begin to adopt new IFRS sustainability standards, we are seeing a growing trend toward valuing long-term resilience over short-term gain. It is high time we applied that same level of rigor to the preservation of our human capital.

### Redefining the Mandate

Investing in the management of non-communicable diseases is not an act of charity. It is a strategic requirement for keeping our economic engine humming. When a worker remains healthy, they continue to support their family and contribute to the national output. Conversely, when preventable complications force them out of the workforce, households are plunged into debt, and the state is left to fund the most expensive, last-minute interventions imaginable.

Moving forward, the success of our reforms will not be determined by the sophistication of our billing software or the complexity of our insurance modules. Success will be measured by how well we manage these silent, chronic killers. Prioritizing funding for early screening and routine management is our only viable path to avoid systemic collapse. A healthier nation will not be built solely with new hospital wings; it will be forged through the persistent, quiet work of health promoters in every village, ensuring that citizens no longer have to fear the cost of staying alive.
