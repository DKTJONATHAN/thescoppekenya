## Grave Politics in Narok Rigathi Gachagua pledges to build mausoleum for late Johana Ngeno is the central subject of the update.

Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua stood over a patch of earth in Mogondo this Friday, offering a checkbook to secure a legacy that the current administration seemingly discarded. While Mary Temas mourned the physical absence of her son, the late MP Johana Ng’eno, the political machinery present suggested this was never just about a tombstone.

### The Mausoleum as a Campaign Asset

Gachagua arrived in Narok County with a clear objective: to rebrand the perceived neglect of the late legislator by the Kenya Kwanza regime as a moral failure of President William Ruto. By pledging to fully fund the construction of a permanent mausoleum, Gachagua is not merely performing a charitable act. He is effectively seizing the narrative of the 'forgotten ally.'

The timing of this visit is surgical. By highlighting the government’s failure to honor promises made to the Ng’eno family, Gachagua positions himself as the only consistent partner for the Rift Valley grassroots. He is utilizing the Democracy for Citizens Party (DCP) as a vessel to bypass the current power structures that have sought to sideline him since his exit from the executive. The DCP is no longer just a legal entity; it is becoming a rallying point for those who feel the promise of 2022 has been hollowed out.

This is a classic play in the Kenyan political playbook: occupy the vacuum left by an incumbent’s arrogance. When a president stops looking back to pay respects to the fallen who helped him rise, those outside the inner circle will always be waiting to pick up the shovel.

### Security and Symbolism in Emurua Dikirr

The heavy security presence during the DCP rally in Emurua Dikirr signaled that this was a high-stakes provocation. Gachagua is testing the limits of his influence in a region that was once the bedrock of Ruto’s support. Every brick laid in the future mausoleum will carry the weight of a protest vote, transforming a site of mourning into a site of resistance.

The optics are unmistakable. By coordinating the design and cost estimates with the bereaved family, Gachagua is ensuring a long-term presence in the region. He is embedding himself into the local identity of Narok, forcing the administration to choose between engaging with his challenge or appearing indifferent to the memory of their own former cohorts.

Similar maneuvers in other spheres demonstrate how quickly power shifts when leadership neglects its foundations. Much like the pivot toward projects like the Aliko Dangote refinery in Mombasa, which reconfigures economic alliances away from traditional political gatekeepers, this mausoleum project seeks to reconfigure local loyalty. It is a quiet form of infrastructure building that pays dividends in votes rather than interest.

### A Debt Left Unpaid

The discourse surrounding the mausoleum centers on broken promises. The administration speaks of national development, but Gachagua speaks of personal betrayal. By framing the government’s silence as a character flaw, he forces the average voter in Emurua Dikirr to weigh the national agenda against their personal connection to their fallen representative.

If the state cannot be trusted to honor the dead, how can it be trusted to look after the living? This is the question Gachagua is banking on as he pivots his focus toward the 2027 horizon. He is betting that the grievances of a family and the memory of an MP can do more to unsettle the status quo than any parliamentary motion.

As the designs for the memorial are finalized in the coming weeks, one must ask: is the state losing its hold on the Rift Valley simply because it stopped attending the funerals of its friends?
