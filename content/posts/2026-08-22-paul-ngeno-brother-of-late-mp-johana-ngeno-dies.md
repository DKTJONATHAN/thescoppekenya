## The Cost of Care in the Wake of Tragedy

Paul Ng'eno passed away on August 21 while receiving treatment in Eldoret, bringing a painful conclusion to a long battle with illness. His death, occurring just 174 days after his brother, MP Johana Ng'eno, perished in a Nandi County helicopter crash, highlights the profound fragility of household stability when faced with back-to-back crises.

### Why does a Ksh 1.8 million hospital bill reveal deeper systemic gaps?

The financial burden faced by the Ng'eno family serves as a sharp reminder of the precarious nature of healthcare in Kenya. When Paul Ng'eno was hospitalized, the bill climbed to Ksh 1.8 million, an amount that would be ruinous for most Kenyan families, regardless of their proximity to political circles. Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua intervened on the day of Paul's passing, pledging to settle these costs. While such interventions are often viewed as gestures of political goodwill, they underscore a systemic failure: the lack of a reliable public insurance safety net that functions without the need for high-profile rescue missions.

When medical costs can only be managed through the benevolence of external donors or political figures, the system effectively denies citizens their right to affordable, dignified care. Even families who appear to have status find themselves at the mercy of private hospital billing cycles, where the clock is always ticking. This dependence on ad-hoc fundraising or political charity demonstrates that our national social health insurance framework is not yet capable of shielding families from the sudden, catastrophic costs that accompany chronic illness.

### How does the 174-day timeline reflect a pattern of family trauma?

The rapid succession of these losses creates a timeline of compounding trauma. Between the February 28 helicopter accident that took the life of the Emurua Dikirr legislator and the passing of his brother in late August, the surviving family members have had little time to grieve or stabilize their affairs. This 174-day window is a microcosm of the exhaustion families face when dealing with sudden death, followed immediately by the complex logistics of long-term medical care.

Many families across the country experience similar cycles, where the loss of a primary breadwinner leads to financial instability, which in turn leaves surviving relatives vulnerable to mounting hospital fees. When resources are depleted, the quality of care often suffers, and families are forced to choose between dignity and bankruptcy. For the Ng'eno family, the tragedy in Nandi County was not merely an isolated accident; it acted as the beginning of a larger struggle against both grief and economic pressure.

### Why does the reliance on political intervention matter to the average citizen?

In the current Kenyan climate, the expectation that political leaders will clear medical debts has become a normalized part of the healthcare landscape. However, this reliance creates a dangerous perception that healthcare is a favor to be granted rather than a service to be expected. As seen with recent infrastructure struggles-such as the operational challenges within the SHA systems and the ongoing frustrations with transport regulations-when the state fails to provide consistent, automated support, citizens are forced to seek help through personal connections.

The death of Paul Ng'eno is a reminder that personal tragedy often exposes the gaps in public service delivery. If a household connected to the political elite still faces the threat of medical bankruptcy until the very end, the reality for the average citizen in Chepkiep Village or elsewhere in the country is infinitely more precarious. True progress in healthcare will not be found in the intermittent payment of bills by leaders, but in building a system where no family is forced to count the days of a loved one's illness against the mounting total of a hospital invoice.

No family should rely on the generosity of political leaders to survive a health crisis.
