## Mombasa’s Offshore Energy Secession Greek Firm Proposes Ksh194 Billion AI Data Centre in Mombasa is the central subject of the update.

Amaco Energy Group is seeking state approval for a Ksh194 billion data centre in Mombasa, signaling a shift toward energy-independent tech hubs. By opting for a dedicated offshore LNG-powered generation system, the Greek corporation intends to bypass the national grid, highlighting a growing friction between decentralized digital infrastructure and Kenya’s centralized transmission strategy.

The proposal arrives as the state pushes its Digital Superhighway agenda, yet it quietly undermines the long-term utility model. While the government prioritizes grid stability through KETRACO’s partnerships with Africa50 and the Power Grid Corporation of India to manage cross-country transmission, private firms are increasingly viewing the national power supply as a liability. By generating its own electricity rather than feeding into the existing network, Amaco effectively walls off its high-demand infrastructure from the load-sharing burdens that define the Olkaria-centered power ecosystem.

### The Grid vs. The Sovereign Hub

This pivot represents a fundamental departure from the traditional industrial development path where heavy users subsidize the expansion of public infrastructure. If Mombasa becomes a sanctuary for self-sufficient digital fortresses, the state risks a hollowed-out utility base. Kenya Power typically relies on high-volume industrial consumers to sustain the balance sheet, yet these new data centres prefer isolation. The Ksh194 billion injection is framed as a win for foreign investment, but it creates a parallel reality where high-end tech giants enjoy reliable power while the rest of the country navigates the limitations of aging transmission corridors.

The tension is visible in the geography of the project. While inland corridors like Kiambere and Maua remain tied to the state-led grid expansion, coastal zones are uniquely positioned to leverage offshore LNG, allowing companies to essentially secede from the national transmission mandate. This choice leaves the state with the responsibility of maintaining lines for residential and smaller commercial users, while the primary drivers of digital growth opt for independent energy loops. It is a configuration that benefits the coastal industrial zone but complicates the broader ambition of a unified national power grid.

### The Legacy of Infrastructure Drift

*   **Mid-2025:** The government formalized the Digital Superhighway framework, aiming to expand fiber reach across 100,000 kilometres while consolidating transmission oversight under KETRACO to stabilize power flow for upcoming tech zones.
*   **Early 2026:** Guodong Group announced a Ksh38.8 billion investment into local digital infrastructure, signaling the start of a competitive rush for land and energy access in coastal regions.
*   **August 2026:** Amaco Energy Group submitted its formal proposal for the Ksh194 billion data centre, formalizing the move toward energy-independent, offshore-powered AI facilities in Mombasa.

The trajectory of such investments often ends in a fragmented infrastructure landscape where public and private power systems rarely intersect. As lending rates move toward an average of 14.38 percent, the appetite for massive, self-contained capital projects like the Amaco proposal grows because it avoids the variable risks of the national grid. For the developers, the benefit is guaranteed uptime; for the state, it is an admission that the existing infrastructure cannot meet the insatiable power appetite of global AI operations.

This offshore model ensures that the data centres remain unaffected by the recurring stability issues plaguing the interior. However, it forces a difficult question: how does the national grid survive when the biggest consumers become their own providers? The state is effectively inviting a future where digital sovereignty is purchased through energy autonomy, leaving the public grid to manage the rising costs of an increasingly disconnected network. Autonomy for the few often accelerates the obsolescence of the many.
