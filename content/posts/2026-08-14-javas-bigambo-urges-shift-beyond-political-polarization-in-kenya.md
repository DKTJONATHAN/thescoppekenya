## The Economic Architecture of Our Stagnation Javas Bigambo Urges Shift Beyond Political Polarization in Kenya is the central subject of the update.

Kenya remains a prisoner of its own history. We are shackled to an economic roadmap drafted in 1965 that has long outlived its utility. Today, this outdated framework serves only to facilitate the systematic looting of our national potential.

Governance expert Javas Bigambo has issued a stark warning: we must abandon the cycles of tribal mobilization that define our current political theater. He contends that our trajectory is not merely hampered by mediocre leadership, but by a dogmatic adherence to Sessional Paper No. 10 of 1965. By prioritizing growth solely through investments in areas deemed to have the highest immediate returns, this document inadvertently institutionalized regional inequality and the competitive tribalism that fuels our toxic election-based polarization.

### The 1965 Trap Versus Modern Reality

Contrast our stagnation with the post-war miracle of Japan. That nation pivoted from total ruin to industrial dominance by ruthlessly discarding archaic economic blueprints. Japan prioritized technology, human capital, and export-led growth. Kenya, conversely, clings to a mid-sixties governance philosophy that treats development as a finite pie to be carved up by political patronage.

This is the primary reason Vision 2030 feels like a distant fantasy. The failure is not in the vision itself, but in the foundational machinery of our state. Our institutions are built for political survival rather than economic production. Whether under the current Kenya Kwanza administration or the regimes that preceded it, the prevailing tendency is to treat development as a political concession. This structural flaw ensures that whenever political temperatures rise, the economy is the immediate casualty. We are attempting to build a high-speed digital economy on a rusted chassis designed for a colonial-era administrative state.

### Beyond The Tribal Mobilization Cycle

The ongoing infighting between President William Ruto and his deputy, Rigathi Gachagua, is a transparent battle for political dominance. This myopia is a symptom of a deeper, systemic rot. If we persist in treating development as a mere byproduct of political settlement, we will remain trapped in this loop of periodic agitation. The proposed New Development Charter, as Bigambo advocates, offers a necessary escape from this transactional model.

True industrial modernization demands a cold, hard decoupling of the national budget from political patronage. If we continue to link school capitation increases or infrastructure projects-such as the recent adjustments in per-learner funding-to the benevolence of politicians rather than a codified, permanent economic rule, we are guaranteeing instability. Modernization requires that we look toward the global market, not the next ballot box. Much like public figures who have had to reconcile with their past deceptions, such as the well-documented incident where King Kaka admitted to forging academic documents, our nation must embrace radical transparency. We must acknowledge that our current political culture is a fraud perpetrated against our own future.

### The Charter for a New Kenya

Critics will argue that politics is the only language the Kenyan electorate understands. This is a cynical trap designed to keep us subservient. The public’s hunger for better living standards is evident in the intense scrutiny of everyday costs and essential social services. It is a mistake to believe that minor social distractions, such as the recent discourse surrounding the ethics of pet surgical procedures, indicate a lack of seriousness in the populace. People care about the economy because they are starving for results, not because they are inherently obsessed with tribal narratives.

We do not require another political movement; we need a policy mandate that outlasts the five-year cycle of our political class. The transition from the antiquated Sessional Paper No. 10 to a modern commercial charter is the only way to stop the bleeding. We must replace the politics of the "Big Man" with the physics of a big economy. Unless we treat our industrial policy with the same gravity as our election results, we will still be waiting for Vision 2030 long after the year 2030 has passed. We are not failing because we are divided. We are divided because our development model forces us to fight for the scraps of a dying vision.
