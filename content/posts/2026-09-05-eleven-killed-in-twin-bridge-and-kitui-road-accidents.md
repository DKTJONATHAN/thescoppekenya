While authorities often point to human error, the recurring nature of these accidents suggests that the design of this stretch is inherently hostile to safe transit. The NTSA continues to emphasize regulatory oversight, yet their mandate to identify and rectify such danger zones appears stalled. For the thousands of Kenyans who rely on this route for daily transport, the highway remains a high-stakes gamble where engineering flaws meet a lack of proactive oversight.

While the Mau Summit incident highlights the risks of major transit corridors, the crash at Kasarani in Kitui County demonstrates the danger lurking on rural, under-maintained road networks. A lorry transporting traders overturned on the Ikutha-Mutulu-Kasaala road, resulting in five deaths and leaving twenty-one others injured. Unlike the Nakuru-Eldoret highway, where speed is often the primary focus of regulators, the Kitui tragedy points to the intersection of poor rural road surfacing and the over-reliance on cargo vehicles to move passengers.

### Why the 2,150 fatality mark is a indictment of policy

Current transport laws and the existing regulatory framework appear increasingly detached from the reality on the ground. Despite the NTSA reporting a significant rise in deaths, the enforcement of vehicle inspections and night-travel guidelines remains reactive rather than preventive. Raphael Mutiso and other safety experts argue that if the government continues to treat these incidents as individual accidents rather than systemic service failures, the death toll will only continue to rise.

The focus on individual driver negligence overlooks the reality that many Kenyan commuters are effectively forced into unsafe vehicles because there is no safer, regulated alternative. True reform requires moving beyond punitive measures against drivers toward a comprehensive overhaul of road geometry and a mandatory investment in emergency medical services along the country's most hazardous routes. Until policy reflects the danger inherent in these identified black spots, the cycle of tragedy on Kenyan roads will persist.

Road safety must be treated as a basic public service rather than an elusive expectation for the traveling public.
