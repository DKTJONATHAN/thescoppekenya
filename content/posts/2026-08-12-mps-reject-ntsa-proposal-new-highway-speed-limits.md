## Prioritizing Speed Over Survival MPs Reject NTSA Proposal for New Speed Limits on Major Highways is the central subject of the update.

The National Assembly Transport Committee has effectively neutered the National Transport and Safety Authority, blocking proposed speed limit reductions under the guise of traffic efficiency. While motorists cheer the absence of tighter restrictions, the data-driven reality of our road fatalities is being buried under political posturing.

The NTSA recently presented a technical proposal to lower speed limits on major arteries, supported by evidence gathered from its 1,000-camera surveillance network. The objective was simple: force a reduction in kinetic energy during accidents to curb the rising death toll on highways like Thika Road and Mombasa Road. Instead of evaluating the proposal against the grim statistics of the last twelve months, the parliamentary committee dismissed the plan as a catalyst for gridlock.

### The Myth of Congestion as a Shield

There is a glaring conflict of interest when the people who populate the highways in high-speed, fuel-guzzling convoys are the same ones regulating the enforcement of road safety laws. By framing speed limits as a mere inconvenience for commuters, the committee successfully pivoted the conversation away from the carnage recorded on our tarmac. The lawmakers argued that fluid movement is the priority, conveniently ignoring the fact that excessive speed is a primary factor in the severity of road accidents.

Data from the NTSA surveillance system clearly maps the correlation between speed and impact lethality. On sections of Mombasa Road where traffic flows at speeds exceeding 110 km/h, the survival rate in the event of a collision drops precipitously. Yet, the committee’s response was not to demand better enforcement or infrastructure upgrades; it was to preserve the current, lethal status quo. It is a classic move in the theatre of populist governance: appease the motorist’s desire for convenience while leaving the victims of road accidents to be measured only in statistics.

### Who Controls the Narrative of Safety?

The rejection of the NTSA proposal signals a shift in who gets to define safety in this country. The authority argues for an evidence-based approach where surveillance data informs policy. Parliament, however, prefers anecdotal evidence-the disgruntled voter who sits in traffic-over the cold, hard numbers captured by high-definition lenses. This creates a regulatory paralysis. If the NTSA cannot adjust limits based on changing road conditions, the 1,000 cameras currently watching the highways become little more than expensive ornaments.

The tension mirrors broader administrative struggles seen elsewhere, much like the recent [Senegal leadership rift](https://zandani.co.ke/article/senegal-leadership-rift-between-faye-and-sonko) where governing mandates clash with public perception. Here, the struggle is between the regulator’s duty to save lives and the representative’s duty to keep their constituents moving, even if that movement is inherently dangerous. While the cost of digital financial tools like those provided by [Tala](https://zandani.co.ke/article/tala-to-cut-10-percent-of-its-workforce-in-kenya) is subject to the harsh winds of market forces, road safety is being treated as a negotiable commodity subject to the whims of an election cycle.

The committee has demanded that the NTSA find alternative safety improvements that do not hamper traffic flow. It is a tall order that effectively asks for a miracle: how to make highways safer without actually slowing down the vehicles that make them lethal. By rejecting these limits, the lawmakers have effectively prioritized the pace of the commute over the sanctity of human life.

When the next spike in highway fatalities is recorded, will the committee members accept responsibility for the policy they chose to block, or will they simply call for another report to deflect the blame?
