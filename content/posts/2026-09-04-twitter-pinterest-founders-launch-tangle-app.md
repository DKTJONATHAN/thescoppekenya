## Beyond the Digital Archive

The launch of Tangle by Biz Stone and Evan Sharp marks a shift toward intentional, ephemeral communication, challenging the assumption that our digital histories must be preserved forever. This platform prioritizes immediate connection while ensuring that every conversation vanishes once the participants move on.

Tangle arrives as an attempt to fix a fundamental tension in modern networking. For years, the social media industry operated on the belief that everything users posted should exist permanently to maximize engagement and advertising revenue. By building a tool that intentionally deletes data, the founders of Twitter and Pinterest are betting that users are exhausted by the weight of their own digital footprints. In Kenya, where the culture of taking screenshots has turned casual remarks into long-term political or social liabilities, this design philosophy acts as a direct challenge to the way we share information. If an app removes the evidence of a conversation as soon as it ends, it theoretically removes the ammunition used in the constant cycle of online disputes that often turn toxic.

### The Conflict of Screenshot Culture

The Kenyan digital experience is defined by a deep-seated reliance on high-permanence platforms. WhatsApp groups serve as the primary infrastructure for everything from family planning to professional networking, creating a massive, searchable database of our private lives. When a message is sent in a group, it stays there, becoming a permanent reference point that can be resurrected months later to embarrass or discredit someone. 

Tangle attempts to disrupt this by mandating that users be present to participate. There is no scrolling through past history because there is no history to scroll through. This ephemeral model threatens the local habit of hoarding digital proof. While some see this as a necessary evolution for privacy, others worry it undermines the accountability that comes with recorded discourse. In a society that is increasingly digitizing public services-much like the recent push for mobile payment integration at the NTSA-the desire for a space that is intentionally forgetful seems counterintuitive. There is a strong skepticism among local analysts regarding whether an app built on scarcity of data can survive in a market where data-heavy, persistent platforms are the default for daily survival.

### Designing for Human Presence

The mechanics of Tangle revolve around scheduled group chats and synchronous interaction. Unlike platforms that allow users to catch up on missed threads, Tangle forces a specific timing for participation. This intentionality is meant to curb the addiction patterns common in apps designed to keep people glued to their screens for hours. By limiting the time window for interaction, the founders hope to replicate the fleeting nature of real-life conversations.

However, the barrier to entry remains high. Despite the pedigree of its creators, the app currently sits with a modest fifty downloads and a small pool of user ratings. For most Kenyans, a move to such a platform requires moving away from the ecosystem of WhatsApp, which is zero-rated by many local mobile providers. When the cost of data is a constant consideration, the utility of a messaging app is often tied to its integration with legacy systems. A privacy-focused tool that demands constant, real-time attention may struggle to compete with the sheer convenience of a system that allows users to reply at their own pace while maintaining a long-term archive of their social circles.

### A Pattern of Ephemerality

This is not the first time a major tech firm has experimented with the idea of fading digital signals. We have seen similar attempts by smaller players to offer secure communication, though most fail to achieve mass adoption because they isolate users from their core professional and social contacts. The trend in the Kenyan market remains anchored to massive, multi-purpose platforms that double as utility providers. If Tangle is to succeed, it must prove that the value of being forgotten outweighs the comfort of being able to track every digital step we take. History suggests that while privacy-focused models garner interest, they often remain as niche experiments rather than replacements for the permanent digital infrastructure of daily life.

New tools often prioritize the user experience of the present, but the habit of looking backward is hard to break.
