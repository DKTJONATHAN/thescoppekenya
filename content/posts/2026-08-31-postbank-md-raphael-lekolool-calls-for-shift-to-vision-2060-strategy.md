## The National Savings Void

**Kenya will remain a beggar nation until we stop consuming our own capital and start locking it into domestic investment.**

Postbank Managing Director Raphael Lekolool is pitching the transition from the expiring Vision 2030 to a new, ambitious Vision 2060 framework. While bureaucrats debate the merits of long-term planning, the hard numbers tell a story of systemic failure. Kenya currently retains a domestic savings rate of just 12 to 15 percent of GDP. This is an embarrassing shortfall when held against the 20 percent average seen across Sub-Saharan Africa. We are not just failing to grow; we are failing to keep the wealth we create within our own borders.

### The Debt Trap of Empty Coffers

This savings gap is the primary reason our national fiscal health is held hostage by foreign creditors. When citizens do not save, the banking system lacks the internal liquidity to fund our own infrastructure or business growth. Consequently, the state is forced to seek expensive, high-interest debt from international lenders to keep the wheels of government turning. 

This cycle mirrors the fragility seen in the broader commercial sector. Just as the Registrar of Companies moves to dissolve 69 businesses, we see the ripple effects of a economy that lacks a stable capital foundation. When companies cannot access affordable local credit, they wither. If we cannot move past the habit of relying on external bailouts to bridge our investment deficits, Vision 2060 will be nothing more than a colorful brochure collecting dust in a ministry basement.

### Digitization as the Engine of Mobilization

Lekolool argues that the path forward lies in technology-led financial mobilization. For years, financial inclusion was framed as merely giving people access to bank accounts and mobile wallets. That was only the first step. True inclusion is now about turning those active accounts into conduits for long-term domestic savings. If we can leverage the same digital platforms that currently facilitate instant consumption to incentivize consistent, automated saving, we could bridge the 8 percent gap that separates us from our neighbors.

The skepticism remains warranted. We have seen policy frameworks promised before, yet the actual mechanisms for wealth retention often fall apart under the weight of inflation and poor execution. If the state continues to pursue high-tax policies that erode disposable income, there will be nothing left to save, regardless of how efficient the mobile apps become. Digital tools are not magic wands; they are infrastructure that requires a disciplined, pro-savings fiscal environment to function.

### A Choice Between Independence and Reliance

The choice for our economic future is binary. We can continue to fuel our lifestyle through debt and consumption, effectively subsidizing our own slow-motion decline. Or, we can treat the domestic savings rate as the most vital indicator of our national sovereignty. If we choose the latter, the focus must shift entirely from the abstract goals of 2060 to the immediate task of financial discipline. 

Government rhetoric about development means nothing if we do not possess the capital to build it ourselves. We are currently watching the consequences of poor financial planning everywhere, from the corporate graveyard of dissolved firms to the persistent unrest of political leaders targeting their own territory instead of fostering unity for economic growth. If we want a different outcome by 2060, we have to start by keeping more than 15 percent of our earnings at home. Prosperity is not something you borrow; it is something you set aside.
