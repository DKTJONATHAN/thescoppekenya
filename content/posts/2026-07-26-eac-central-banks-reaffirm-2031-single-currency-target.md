## Bridging the Fiscal Abyss: The EAC Monetary Union Ambition

East African Community central bank governors confirmed a 2031 launch for a regional single currency on Friday despite admitting that none of the member states currently satisfy the mandatory macroeconomic stability requirements necessary for a functional monetary union.

The Monetary Affairs Committee meeting held this week highlighted a deepening disconnect between the bloc's political timetable and the fiscal performance of individual nations. To qualify for the union, member states must maintain a headline inflation rate of no more than 8 percent, a fiscal deficit of 3 percent or less of GDP, and tax-to-GDP ratios of 25 percent. Additionally, states are required to maintain a reserve cover of at least 4.5 months of imports. Current data indicates that every nation in the bloc is falling short of at least two of these benchmarks, with sovereign debt levels across the region rising sharply since 2024.

This persistence toward the 2031 deadline mirrors the long-standing tension seen in other sectors, much like the intense policy shifts observed in how leaders like William Ruto evolved from his earlier roles as a strategist to a tax-focused presidency. The governors are attempting to bypass these systemic failures by pivoting toward a new, strictly enforced peer review mechanism. Unlike previous surveillance attempts that relied on voluntary compliance, this new framework will require quarterly reports submitted directly to the regional secretariat, theoretically forcing laggard economies to disclose budget gaps that were previously obscured by opaque accounting practices.

### The Debt Reality Check

The feasibility of the 2031 goal faces significant pressure from the current sovereign debt crisis. Several EAC nations are allocating more than 40 percent of their annual tax revenue to debt servicing, leaving minimal room for the fiscal consolidation required by the union criteria. Financial analysts argue that the commitment to a common currency, while attractive for trade facilitation, ignores the reality that these economies are currently out of sync. Without a mechanism for fiscal transfers from wealthier states to poorer ones, critics warn that a single currency could collapse under the weight of regional inequality.

Proponents of the plan point to the EAC Cross-Border Payment System Masterplan as the stabilizing anchor that will provide the necessary infrastructure to manage liquidity. By streamlining how currencies move across borders, the committee hopes to reduce the reliance on external hard currencies like the US dollar. This digital integration is intended to lower the cost of doing business, potentially creating a unified economic bloc that is more attractive to foreign direct investment. However, until the four core convergence criteria are met, the payment system remains a tactical fix for a structural problem.

### Institutional Skepticism and Future Hurdles

Regional economists remain cautious about the governors' rigid adherence to the timeline. Many suggest that the project has become a victim of its own political narrative, where the goal of integration takes precedence over the technical prerequisites for stability. The fear among private sector players is that the transition could lead to volatile interest rates as central banks struggle to harmonize policies across disparate economic landscapes. If the central banks are forced to abandon their domestic mandates to support a regional target that the member states are not prepared for, the resulting instability could stifle growth rather than stimulate it.

Despite these hurdles, the governors have signaled that they will not seek an extension beyond the 2031 target. The focus for the remainder of the 2026-27 period will shift to the harmonization of legislative frameworks, specifically aiming to align tax laws and public procurement standards. While the political will remains strong at the committee level, the ultimate success of this venture hinges on whether member governments are willing to cede control over their national monetary tools. The next major test will occur in early 2027 when the peer review mechanism delivers its first audit of how member states are correcting their debt trajectories to align with the regional protocol.
