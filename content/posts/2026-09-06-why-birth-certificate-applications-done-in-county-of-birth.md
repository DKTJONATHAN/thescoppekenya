## Why You Must Apply for Birth Certificate in County of Birth

Huduma Kenya on Sunday, September 06, 2026, clarified that parents must process initial birth certificate applications within the specific county where a child was delivered because primary physical records remain archived in local registries. The clarification, issued in Nairobi, addresses widespread inquiries from citizens who face turned-away requests after trying to lodge paperwork in their current towns of residence rather than where the delivery occurred.

### Why can records only be traced locally?

The directive stems from how the Civil Registration Department archives infant delivery documents across Kenya. Whenever a child is born in a medical facility or through an attended home birth in areas such as Nakuru or Nairobi, the health center submits the counterfoil of the birth notification directly to the local sub-county civil registry.

Civil registrars enter these initial notifications into physical ledger books tied to the registration district of birth. Because historical files and physical register books are stored within county-level archive rooms, registry staff stationed in another county cannot physically access or verify the corresponding record book entry.

### What documents and payments are required?

Parents seeking the document must present a specific bundle of primary materials to their birth county's Civil Registration office or the local Huduma Centre. These include the original birth notification slip issued by the attending hospital, the child's infant clinic card, and original national identification cards for both parents. If one or both parents are deceased, official death certificates must be produced in place of the national identity cards.

The statutory application fee is Ksh250 for standard processing. Once the registrar locates the matching entry in the district record books, cross-checks the parental signatures, and confirms the hospital serial numbers, the entry is endorsed and entered into the issuance queue.

### How does this affect parents who have relocated?

Under the current operational rules, internal migration creates logistical hurdles for families. A parent who gave birth in Kisumu or Mombasa and later relocated to Nairobi must either travel back to the county of delivery or designate a family member with authorized power of attorney and certified copies of documents to trace the registry book locally.

Although the state has introduced digital portals to handle new birth notifications, older files and unlinked facility logs still demand physical verification on site. The government has maintained that until legacy paper registries across all 47 counties are completely scanned and linked to a centralized database, cross-county retrieval remains technically unfeasible.

## Analysis

The requirement for parents to travel back to their county of delivery highlights a persistent disconnect between Kenya's public digital transformation and its legacy paper bureaucracy. For years, state agencies have advertised seamless digital public services, yet fundamental vital statistics continue to rely on physical storage rooms, manual ledgers, and localized ink stamps.

This policy creates a heavy financial and time burden on working-class citizens. For a family that relocated across the country, spending thousands of shillings on bus fare to secure a Ksh250 document is an expensive hurdle. Many parents inevitably postpone the exercise until primary school enrollment or national examinations make the certificate urgent, which then creates seasonal overcrowding at county registries.

The situation also shows the uneven progress within civil registration reform. While modern health facilities enter deliveries into electronic systems at birth, millions of older records remain locked in physical volumes that have not been indexed centrally. The Civil Registration Department cannot safely permit nationwide processing without risking duplicate entries, identity theft, or fraudulent registrations.

Until the state finishes digitizing every sub-county registry archive and ties those records to a unified national identity register, citizens will continue to bear the cost of an administrative system rooted in geographic boundaries.
