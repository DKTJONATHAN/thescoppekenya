## The Auctioning of Our Ballot Need for stricter financial oversight of political party fees is the central subject of the update.

Political parties often claim that nomination fees are merely a necessary mechanism to fund grassroots mobilization and manage internal elections. This comfortable narrative is a lie designed to shield an industry that has morphed into a private enterprise where democratic participation is traded for cash.

When Emmanuel Sara Bhai received his registration certificate for the People’s Renaissance Movement on April 22, 2026, he became another line item in a growing financial ledger. These transactions occur behind closed doors, hidden from public scrutiny and devoid of any independent oversight. If political parties want to be treated as agents of democracy, they must first account for the millions of shillings they harvest from aspirants before a single vote is cast.

### The Profit-Seeking Loophole

The Political Parties Act has inadvertently birthed a class of institutions that prioritize revenue generation over ideological representation. Current regulations are notoriously porous, allowing parties to set fee structures without any requirement to justify the amounts or explain where the capital flows after the primary season concludes. We have seen how financial distress can dismantle public trust, much like the recent exploitation of digital creators whose livelihoods were hijacked, yet we grant political machines a free pass to operate with even less accountability. 

These fees are not administrative costs; they are entry barriers that turn political seats into commodities. When a party sets a six-figure nomination fee, they are not funding democracy; they are weeding out anyone without a wealthy donor base. This is the definition of a closed market, a silent cartel where the price of entry effectively auctions the democratic process to the highest bidder while the public remains locked out of the financial books.

### The Myth of Transparency

There is zero evidence that these massive inflows are being reinvested into the organizational capacity of parties. Instead, we see a recurring pattern where financial data is treated as a state secret. Where are the audited statements showing how the fees paid by hopefuls are allocated? The Office of the Registrar of Political Parties sits on its hands, failing to mandate the disclosure of fee calculation criteria. 

While the state pushes for massive capital projects like the [dualling of the Makutano-Meru highway](https://zandani.co.ke/article/president-ruto-announces-dualling-of-makutano-meru-highway-under-nat), the governance of the very bodies that produce our leaders remains stuck in a pre-modern era of opaque accounting. We demand to know why a political party can operate with the secrecy of a private hedge fund while playing the role of the gatekeeper to our national parliament. If the party machinery cannot prove that its bank accounts are being used for legitimate political activity, then these nomination fees are nothing more than a legalized tax on the ambitions of the citizenry.

### A Call for Immediate Audit

We should stop viewing these parties as noble vehicles for political discourse and start treating them as the commercial ventures they have become. The only way to stop this systemic extortion is to impose strict, mandatory financial reporting requirements. Any party that fails to publish a public, itemized breakdown of how it determines nomination fees and how it spends that revenue should face immediate suspension of its registration. 

True reform requires shifting the burden of proof back onto the parties. The era of the silent cartel must end. If the institution of the party cannot withstand the scrutiny of its own balance sheet, it has no business determining the direction of the country. Stop the auctions and force the books open. Democracy is not a commodity to be sold to the highest bidder at the gate of a party headquarters.
