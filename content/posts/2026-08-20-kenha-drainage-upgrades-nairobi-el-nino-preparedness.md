## Nairobi Expressway Drainage: Can Concrete Defy an 81 Percent Forecast?

Meteorological experts have placed the probability of heavy El Niño rains at 81 percent for the final quarter of 2026. As the Kenya National Highways Authority begins drainage upgrades along the James Gichuru to JKIA corridor, commuters are asking: will these repairs actually hold, or are they merely a temporary fix against a generational weather event?

### Is standard drainage ready for an El Niño surge?

The ongoing work on the expressway corridor involves clearing clogged outlets, widening drainage channels, and reinforcing culverts that were overwhelmed during previous rainy seasons. While these engineering steps are standard maintenance, they are being applied to a landscape that has undergone significant urban densification since the road was designed. Civil engineers often design drainage systems based on historical rainfall patterns, but El Niño events are outliers. They bring intense, short-duration bursts of water that exceed the drainage capacity of most urban roads. If the current upgrades only focus on restoring original flow rates, they may struggle to handle the sheer volume of runoff anticipated between October and December. The strategy appears to favor surface-level maintenance, but the true test remains whether the underground piping network can manage the surge without backing up onto the asphalt.

### Why does the JKIA corridor remain a high-risk zone?

Historically, the section of the road leading toward the airport acts as a catchment area. topography plays a major role here. Much of the water from surrounding developments flows downward, eventually meeting the highway at points where natural drainage paths have been constricted by years of construction. Even with the multi-agency teams mobilized by Governor Johnson Sakaja to monitor infrastructure stability, the volume of water expected during an El Niño period often surpasses the physical capacity of traditional drainage designs. When the drainage is overwhelmed, the result is localized flooding that turns the expressway into a river. The government is banking on these preemptive measures to prevent a repeat of last year's traffic chaos, but the reliance on standard clearing techniques ignores the fact that extreme climate patterns require extreme infrastructure resilience.

### What should motorists expect on the road?

Drivers must prepare for a season where infrastructure capacity will likely be tested to its limit. If you are planning to traverse major highways during the October-December window, keep a few realities in mind. First, road sections prone to ponding will likely flood regardless of recent upgrades if the intensity of the rain is high enough. Second, visibility will drop significantly during these storms, making the speed-limit signs on the expressway secondary to your own judgment. If the water begins to pool, do not assume the road surface is stable underneath. While officials like [Raymond Omollo are pushing for improved security and safety](https://zandani.co.ke/article/raymond-omollo-outlines-plans-to-boost-police-force-and-security), the physical reality of a flooded road remains a danger regardless of the presence of emergency teams. Keep an eye on weather updates and consider altering your commute during heavy downpours, as even the best-drained highways can fail when faced with unprecedented rainfall levels.

The success of these drainage upgrades hinges on whether the engineering can match the extreme meteorological forecast, rather than simply maintaining the status quo. If you notice persistent pooling in areas that have supposedly been cleared, do not expect that water to clear quickly during the peak of the season.

Infrastructure upgrades are only as strong as their ability to handle the next unexpected weather peak.
