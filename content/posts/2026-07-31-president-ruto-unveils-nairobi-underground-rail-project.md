## Can Nairobi Really Dig Its Way Out of Traffic?

President William Ruto confirmed today that his administration is pursuing a 30-kilometre underground rail network connecting Eastlands to the Central Business District. While the vision promises to move millions, history suggests that shifting Nairobi's transit burden beneath the earth is a gamble that ignores the city's difficult terrain.

### Is the Ksh1 trillion price tag realistic for our economy?

Governor Johnson Sakaja has placed the initial cost of the project at Ksh1 trillion. To put this figure in perspective, the total national budget for the current fiscal year reflects the severe constraints currently facing the Treasury. Recently, the government initiated an early budget cycle and placed a strict ban on new pending bills, signaling a desperate need for fiscal discipline. Financing a project of this magnitude requires a debt-to-GDP ratio that Kenya has struggled to maintain, especially when previous major infrastructure works like the 2014 commuter rail upgrades faced massive cost overruns. The Ksh388 billion spent on existing flagship projects provides a cautionary tale. If the state cannot comfortably manage its current liabilities, the capital required for deep-tunnel boring machines and underground station construction could drain resources from essential services for decades.

### How does this project compare to the failed master plans of the past?

Urban planners have chased the dream of a sophisticated Nairobi transport system since 1948, when colonial authorities first mapped out a grid for a growing city. By 1972, a more ambitious master plan identified the need for high-capacity rapid transit to accommodate projected population growth. Every decade since, planners have revived variations of these sketches, yet the city remains trapped in gridlock. The constant shift from one mode of transport to another-from commuter rail to Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) and now to underground tunnels-has left us without a unified network. Where the 1972 vision focused on surface-level efficiency, the current proposal leans toward subterranean solutions. This pivot ignores the lessons of the past: when infrastructure plans change every ten years, the result is usually half-finished concrete skeletons rather than a functional grid.

### Does Nairobi's geology support deep-tunnel construction?

Digging beneath Nairobi is not merely a matter of engineering. The city rests on a complex mix of volcanic rock, weathered phonolites, and unstable sedimentary layers. Constructing 25 underground stations and miles of tunnel requires an environment that does not threaten the stability of the high-rise buildings already looming over the city centre. Previous large-scale excavations in the CBD have often hit high-pressure groundwater tables, leading to dangerous structural shifts. Beyond the geological risks, the project faces a social hurdle. Thousands of matatu operators depend on the surface-level routes connecting Eastlands to town. An underground system threatens their primary source of income, potentially setting the stage for industrial conflict that could stall the project even before the first shovel hits the ground.

While the allure of a sleek, subterranean train is strong, the reliance on high-cost tunnels over surface-level BRT upgrades suggests a preference for prestige over practicality. If the government fails to address the foundational issues of geological risk and debt sustainability, this project risks becoming another expensive entry in a century of stalled transit promises.

Nairobi's transit future should be built on stable ground, not on billion-shilling holes in the mud.
