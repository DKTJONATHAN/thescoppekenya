## The Misplaced Blame of the Period Taboo

The prevailing narrative concerning women’s health in Kenya suggests that the primary barrier to care is a cultural stigma surrounding menstruation. This explanation is far too convenient. By pinning the blame on intangible social taboos, the health sector dodges accountability for a much more dangerous issue: the institutional refusal to treat pathological bleeding as a medical concern. We are failing to distinguish between standard biological cycles and systemic illness, opting to hide behind cultural excuses rather than address the negligence occurring within our clinical walls.

### The Clinical Gaslighting of the Seven-Day Cycle

When a patient seeks care for abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB), the medical response is frequently dismissive. Clinicians often rely on a lazy rule of thumb that defines bleeding lasting up to seven days as normal. This heuristic serves as a gatekeeper, shielding providers from the effort of ordering ultrasounds or blood panels. Calling this "cultural sensitivity" is a disservice to the truth; in practice, it is medical negligence. By reframing a clinical symptom as a social nuisance, frontline workers ensure that conditions like fibroids, polyps, and hormonal imbalances remain undiagnosed. 

The consequences of this diagnostic inertia are severe. Our clinics are flooded with cases of preventable iron deficiency anaemia, yet the response remains tepid. It is estimated that one in three women of reproductive age in Kenya suffers from chronic exhaustion caused by these untreated disorders. The public health focus remains stubbornly fixed on the distribution of sanitary pads-a material solution that ignores the underlying physiological crisis. If patients presented with similar symptoms in respiratory or cardiac medicine, the response would be swift and alarmist. Because the issue is rooted in the uterus, it is treated as a private discomfort rather than a public health failure.

### Universal Health Coverage and the Data Gap

The government’s pursuit of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is fundamentally flawed if reproductive pathology continues to exist in a blind spot. Current data collection efforts prioritize communicable diseases and maternal mortality, yet they are deliberately silent on the morbidity associated with AUB. The logic is simple: if you do not count a condition, you do not have to manage it. By excluding menstrual health metrics from national records, the Ministry of Health effectively signals that the long-term well-being of the female workforce is not a priority.

This administrative indifference is striking when held against other national priorities. The state shows significant vigor in tracking and reporting on everything from vaccine distribution to international sports league progress. We have the technical infrastructure to document minor health trends, yet we lack a national mandate to map the prevalence of anaemia linked to uterine health. As long as AUB remains outside the scope of reportable public health issues, UHC will remain a hollow promise that bypasses the specific, urgent needs of half the population.

### From Cultural Myth to Diagnostic Standard

It is time to discard the "taboo" framing, as it serves only to distract from the real issue. We do not need a public awareness campaign about breaking silences in our living rooms; we need a radical revision of protocols in our examination rooms. The Ministry of Health must enforce mandatory clinical training that requires haemoglobin testing for any woman reporting extended or heavy bleeding, regardless of her history.

Accountability is the missing link. Practitioners who normalize pathological bleeding under the guise of general health must be reined in by updated, rigorous clinical guidelines. The goal is to elevate AUB from a "private issue" to a critical diagnostic marker for overall health. The silence surrounding this crisis is not a product of culture. It is a manufactured outcome of a health system that finds it far more efficient to ignore the patient than to provide the diagnostics they deserve. We must stop treating blood loss as a secret and start treating it as a symptom that requires urgent medical intervention.
