## Commuters Push Back Against Ride-Hailing Price Floors Nairobi riders oppose government proposed minimum fare hikes is the central subject of the update.

A new study reveals that most Nairobi ride-hailing users reject the Ministry of Transport proposal to mandate higher minimum fares, creating a collision between driver welfare initiatives and the purchasing power of daily commuters across the capital.

The latest findings from TIFA Research, based on a survey of 733 Nairobi residents conducted between July 17 and 21, show that 59 percent of users oppose the government-led minimum fare increases. Only 39 percent of respondents support the adjustment, which is designed to raise the current floor of 180 to 220 shillings. The data highlights a disconnect in the policy rollout, as only 27 percent of those surveyed were aware that the government was formalizing these new pricing structures before they participated in the study.

### The Elasticity Conflict

The proposed regulation attempts to address driver grievances regarding stagnant earnings, yet it ignores the fundamental price elasticity of the Nairobi transport market. Economists argue that artificial fare floors effectively shift the cost of labor reforms onto the passenger, risking a significant reduction in demand for ride-hailing services. If costs rise beyond the willingness of the middle-class commuter to pay, the resulting drop in trip volumes could negate the intended income gains for drivers.

Commuters are increasingly concerned that these changes will introduce inflationary pressure into their daily budgets. Many riders view the ride-hailing sector as a utility rather than a luxury, and they fear that the government is prioritizing policy optics over the financial reality of the average city dweller. This tension was palpable during the sector workshops held on August 18 and 19, where stakeholders reviewed the transitional take-home figures before a final submission to the presidency.

### Knowledge Gaps and Market Stability

The low awareness rate of 27 percent suggests that the Ministry of Transport failed to conduct sufficient public consultation before moving toward a policy shift. When citizens are excluded from the deliberative process, they are more likely to perceive resulting fare hikes as arbitrary rather than necessary. This sentiment creates a volatile environment where public trust in both the regulator and the digital platforms is eroding.

Market observers suggest that the current proposal may force a shift back to traditional public transport options if the price floor is set too high. While drivers have long lobbied for state intervention to curb platform commission rates, they may find themselves in a precarious position if the mandate leads to a mass exodus of customers. Balancing the livelihood of gig workers against the economic constraints of the public remains a complex challenge for the current administration.

As stakeholders await the final decision from the executive office, the focus is shifting toward whether the government will incorporate the feedback from riders into the final policy draft. Any successful regulatory overhaul will require a delicate balance between sustainable driver wages and maintaining a fare structure that remains accessible to the broader population. The next phase of policy implementation is expected to clarify whether the state will favor mandated price controls or allow market competition to continue defining the cost of a ride.
