## A Blueprint Built on Borrowed Time Assessing the implementation challenges for Vision 2060 in Kenya is the central subject of the update.

This Sunday morning, I found myself staring at a dust-covered copy of a report from two decades ago, its edges curling in the Nairobi humidity. We are preparing for the grand unveiling of Vision 2060, yet the silence surrounding our past unfulfilled promises feels louder than any upcoming ribbon-cutting ceremony.

History in Kenya often feels like a looping record. We move from one glossy brochure to the next, hoping the ink on the page will somehow alter our material reality. The impending arrival of Vision 2060 brings with it a familiar excitement, but beneath the surface lies the ghost of Sessional Paper No. 10 of 1965. Back then, we articulated a vision for African socialism and rapid growth. Decades later, we find ourselves echoing the same lofty aspirations, while the physical evidence of our stagnation gathers dust in unfinished projects across the counties.

### The Cycle of Paper Ambition

The institutional memory of our civil service seems designed to forget. When we look back at the implementation trajectory of Vision 2030, we see a landscape littered with half-built hospitals and abandoned road networks. The issue is rarely a lack of vision; it is a profound deficiency in follow-through. We treat these documents as literary achievements rather than operational manuals. While our national debt has climbed to over 12 trillion shillings, we continue to produce elaborate policy blueprints that fail to account for the tightening fiscal space or the basic mechanics of public service delivery.

### Lessons from the Lost Years

First, we must acknowledge the gap between rhetoric and the street. When fuel prices spike or public transport comes to a grinding halt, it matters little what a document projected for the year 2060. The citizen on the ground is struggling with the taxes of 2026, not the economic modeling of the future. Our failure to integrate long-term goals with immediate economic realities creates a disconnect that breeds cynicism.

Second, the structural inertia in our government offices acts as a filter that strips ambition of its teeth. Bureaucracy in Kenya has mastered the art of filing reports without shifting gears. Vision 2060 will likely suffer the same fate as its predecessors unless the civil service is incentivized by outcomes rather than output. Publishing a document is an activity, not a result.

Third, we lack an independent mechanism to hold these visions to account. We treat our development plans as suggestions rather than binding commitments. Unless there is a transparent, legally mandated framework to audit progress-with consequences for inertia-we are simply creating another expensive archival item. We see this instability in other areas of life, from the recent frustrations with the tax filing deadlines to the recurring scandals in our athletics programs, where the lack of accountability mirrors the lack of progress in our national infrastructure.

### Building Beyond the Page

If we are serious about a path to 2060, the government must move away from the ritual of the grand launch. We need to stop measuring success by the thickness of a report or the eloquence of a speech. True development is found in the reliability of our power grid, the accessibility of healthcare, and the steady reduction of our debt burden. 

We do not need more dreams of 2060. We need a quiet, relentless focus on the work required to survive 2026. Until we confront why our previous papers remain mostly ornamental, the latest vision remains nothing more than a ghost, haunting a future we have yet to actually build.
