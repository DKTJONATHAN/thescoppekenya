## When Political Mobilization Turns Into Street Violence

The recent disruptions targeting Rigathi Gachagua in Nakuru West have reignited debates over the dangerous intersection of economic desperation and partisan conflict. Beyond the finger-pointing between political heavyweights, the incident highlights a persistent failure to protect vulnerable demographics from being exploited as tools for local power struggles.

### Why do political factions rely on youth foot soldiers?

Nakuru West currently faces significant socio-economic hurdles, with youth unemployment rates hovering at levels that leave many residents susceptible to short-term financial incentives. Local civil society groups argue that this economic vacuum is the primary engine behind political volatility. When leaders view rallies as theaters for dominance rather than platforms for policy debate, they often recruit disenfranchised young men to act as human barriers or provocateurs.

This model of engagement relies on the "mobilization" of groups who feel disconnected from formal employment. By providing small payments or promises of protection, political actors successfully outsource the dirty work of intimidation. For these young people, the day-to-day struggle for survival overrides the long-term consequences of engaging in civic unrest. Local community activists emphasize that as long as the cost of participation remains lower than the opportunity cost of honest work, the cycle of using youth as political proxies will continue to threaten the stability of the region.

### Is the current legal framework failing to stop this?

The Political Parties Act provides clear guidelines regarding the conduct of members and the organization of public events. However, there is a massive gulf between the statutory requirements for peaceful assembly and the reality on the ground. The Directorate of Criminal Investigations consistently draws a line between political mobilization and incitement, yet the enforcement threshold is notoriously difficult to cross in real-time.

Legal experts note that proving direct sponsorship or orchestrating violence requires evidence that often vanishes once the youths disperse. This creates a culture of impunity where leaders can distance themselves from the actions of their supporters by claiming they were absent or unaware. While the law mandates that parties maintain order during campaigns and public rallies, it lacks the mechanisms to hold individual sponsors accountable for the behavior of crowds they have gathered. Until the state begins to penalize the source of funding rather than just the individuals in the street, leaders will continue to treat the law as a suggestion rather than a restriction.

### What are the long-term costs of these proxy wars?

The frequent disruption of political gatherings has consequences that extend well beyond the immediate safety of the politicians involved. When violence becomes an expected component of local rallies, ordinary residents avoid engaging with the political process altogether. This retreat from public discourse weakens the democratic fabric of the county, leaving decisions to those with the loudest-and often most aggressive-voices.

The environment of mistrust fostered by such incidents makes it increasingly difficult for civil society to advocate for actual policy changes, such as the reforms requested by [Kenyan transport stakeholders regarding fuel pricing](https://zandani.co.ke/article/kenya-transport-strike-fuel-prices-protest). When resources are spent managing security threats created by political ego, the local government loses the capacity to address real economic grievances. Similar patterns were observed recently when a [truck was destroyed during protests in Chesumei](https://zandani.co.ke/article/chesumei-mp-paul-biego-truck-destroyed-naivasha-protests), serving as a reminder that the collateral damage of political instability is almost always borne by working-class citizens, not the political elite. The reliance on youth proxies does not just damage a single motorcade; it erodes the safety of every citizen attempting to participate in public life.

Local stability depends on moving past the era where political influence is measured by the number of people a leader can mobilize to disrupt an opponent.
