## Beyond the Chancellor: The Private Engine of Public Degrees

The University of Nairobi confirmed the passing of Dr. Vijoo Rattansi this Thursday, closing a chapter on a decade of stewardship that redirected the institution’s reliance from dwindling state coffers toward the strategic deployment of private endowment capital for student welfare.

### A Decade of Managed Solvency

Dr. Rattansi served as Chancellor from 2013 to 2023, but her influence on the University of Nairobi started long before her formal appointment. As the driving force behind the Rattansi Educational Trust, she shifted the narrative of Kenyan higher education funding by treating student bursaries as an essential infrastructure project rather than mere charity. During her tenure, the trust scaled its interventionist model, providing predictable liquidity to students who found themselves unable to clear tuition fees as the government transitioned toward a model of higher student cost-sharing.

The financial data from the last twenty years paints a stark picture of this shift. Since 2006, the trust has consistently acted as a secondary lender for the most vulnerable cohorts within the university system. By managing a diversified portfolio, Dr. Rattansi ensured the trust remained immune to the fiscal volatility that frequently plagued public university budgets. While the government debated budget allocations and payroll audits, the trust functioned as an agile, private backstop, keeping thousands of students enrolled who otherwise would have been forced to defer their studies.

### Timeline of a Funding Evolution

- 2006: The Rattansi Educational Trust formalized its expanded bursary framework, setting a precedent for private philanthropic entities to fill systemic gaps in public university funding.
- 2013: Dr. Vijoo Rattansi is appointed Chancellor of the University of Nairobi, signaling a move toward integrating board-level management with university financial oversight.
- 2023: Concluding her chancellorship, Dr. Rattansi leaves behind a template for endowment-driven education that is currently being studied as the government maneuvers to launch a sustainability-linked sovereign bond to shore up public infrastructure.

### The Institutional Dependence Trap

The reliance on private trusts to sustain public institutions is a double-edged sword. While beneficiaries-such as recent engineering graduates who credit the trust for their final-year stability-praise the tangible relief provided, critics point to the systemic risk inherent in this model. When the state retreats from full funding, the burden falls on private philanthropic entities to maintain access. Dr. Rattansi navigated this by professionalizing the trust, moving away from erratic donations toward a structured investment approach.

This shift mirrors a broader trend seen across various sectors in Kenya, from sports finance where private groups sustain league operations, to the tech industry where developers rely on private global settings to optimize local performance. Just as a phone user might tweak Samsung Galaxy settings to force efficiency on aging hardware, the university system has become adept at squeezing maximum output from private endowments when public funding falters. However, this creates a structural vulnerability: the education of the next generation of professionals becomes tethered to the longevity and investment success of specific family trusts.

The economic reality is that the public university system has moved away from a state-sponsored model toward one managed by proxy. The legacy of Dr. Rattansi is not merely the number of students who graduated under her watch, but the uncomfortable reality that public education now functions on the benevolence of private foundations. As the university moves into its next phase of leadership, the dependence on these external capital buffers will likely become more pronounced. Institutional history suggests that when private philanthropy becomes the backbone of public service, the state eventually loses the incentive to reclaim the financial responsibility it once held.
