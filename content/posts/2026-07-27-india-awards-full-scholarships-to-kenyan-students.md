## The Export of Potential India Awards 105 Fully Funded Scholarships to Kenyan Students is the central subject of the update.

The Indian High Commission recently announced 105 fully funded scholarships for Kenyan students. While many laud this as a necessary infusion of technical expertise for national progress, such optimism is arguably premature. Celebrating the availability of foreign seats obscures a deeper, more uncomfortable reality: our systemic inability to retain the very talent we claim to prioritize.

### The Myth of Returning Talent

Public discourse often insists that dispatching our brightest minds to study artificial intelligence and cybersecurity abroad creates a pipeline to solve domestic tech challenges. This perspective suffers from a fundamental blind spot regarding the actual state of our employment market. Kenya currently struggles to keep homegrown professionals within its borders, primarily because the domestic sector remains hampered by chronic underfunding and a lack of sophisticated industrial projects. 

Sending a gifted developer to immerse themselves in a booming global tech hub does not guarantee their return. It is arguably counterproductive. These students will eventually graduate into a local job market where digital transformation often functions as a hollow buzzword used to describe the digitization of bureaucratic inefficiencies. 

The gap between our educational output and our actual industrial absorption capacity is wide. By framing these 105 scholarships as a vital bridge to a digital future, we ignore the fact that our local infrastructure is not yet equipped to utilize such specialized knowledge. Graduates returning from prestigious foreign institutions frequently find that the most viable local paths are either limited to entry-level administrative support or the precarious gig economy. In effect, we are providing the initial training costs for foreign economies, which stand ready to recruit our top graduates the moment they finish their studies.

### Circular Migration or Permanent Exile

Diplomatic rhetoric regarding these scholarships relies on the theory of circular migration-the idea that knowledge will naturally flow back to the country of origin to fuel development. This model is only successful if the home country provides a professional environment capable of competing with the global market. Without competitive compensation and a mature, robust tech ecosystem, we are not facilitating knowledge exchange. Instead, we are merely subsidizing a permanent brain drain. 

Much like the systemic disconnect between digital streaming success and actual artist compensation, these scholarships create a grand illusion of development. The prestige of an international degree cannot mask the lack of domestic opportunity. 

We must abandon the notion that foreign education is a viable surrogate for necessary domestic policy reform. If the objective is to address gaps in critical fields like engineering, agriculture, and healthcare, the priority must shift to building local capacity. Real development requires structural, institutional change-the kind that makes Kenya the most attractive destination for our own people. Similar to how regional neighbors are pursuing market-based institutional reforms to manage human capital, we need to address the hemorrhage of talent rather than applying a temporary bandage. These scholarships provide a superficial sense of progress while the underlying causes of our technological deficit remain untouched.

### The Cost of Complacency

We should be asking why our domestic institutions are failing to produce these specialists and why our private sector cannot incentivize their retention. We have become remarkably adept at marketing our human capital to the rest of the world, effectively turning the development of our youth into a form of export-led commodity trade. 

Until the focus shifts from accumulating foreign scholarships to creating meaningful professional pathways that reward expertise, these initiatives will continue to serve as a sophisticated mechanism for losing our best citizens. We must stop viewing education as a diplomatic favor and start treating it as a strategic asset. To cultivate our potential, we must ensure that the environment at home is as promising as the education we seek abroad. Exporting our brightest minds should not be our primary strategy for development. Instead, we should be building a nation that does not require them to leave in order to succeed.
