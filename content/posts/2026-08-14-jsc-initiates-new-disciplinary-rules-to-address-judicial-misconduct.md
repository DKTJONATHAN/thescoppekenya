## The Gavel’s Long-Awaited Accountability Check JSC Initiates New Disciplinary Rules to Address Judicial Miscondu is the central subject of the update.

Disillusioned litigants are calling for more than just paperwork as the Judicial Service Commission drafts new disciplinary rules to penalize misconduct short of removal. The move attempts to address a deep-seated culture of perceived impunity that has long stifled the courtroom experience.

The Judicial Service Commission is currently drafting a regulatory framework intended to police judicial officers for misconduct that does not reach the threshold for immediate dismissal. This shift follows weeks of intense friction between the commission and the Law Society of Kenya, which recently staged a national court boycott to protest systemic delays and integrity issues. While the commission engages the National Assembly to solidify these new disciplinary mandates, the focus remains on closing the gap between administrative oversight and the daily frustrations of those seeking justice in Kenyan courtrooms.

### Voices from the Queue

For those standing on the outside looking in, the new rules are being met with cautious skepticism. John Mutua, a small-scale trader who has spent two years chasing a property dispute, views the announcement through the lens of economic survival. He explains that every adjournment is not just a lost day in court, but a lost day of income for his family. For him, the proposed disciplinary measures feel abstract because they do not guarantee that his case will move any faster. He argues that real accountability would be measured by how many cases are actually finalized, rather than how many judges receive a warning letter in a closed office.

Inside the legal fraternity, the sentiment is equally heavy. Sarah Wanjiru, a junior advocate who represents clients in civil matters, notes that the current mechanisms for reporting judicial delay or bias are opaque and often feel like a dead end. She points out that lawyers are afraid to flag misconduct because they fear retaliation against their current and future clients. According to her, if these new rules do not provide a transparent, third-party avenue for lodging complaints, the commission is simply moving the goalposts without changing the game. She remains convinced that internal processes alone cannot clean up the institution without a public-facing watchdog.

Public observers are also weighing in, noting that the relationship between the bench and the bar has reached a breaking point. Many citizens see the recent boycotts as a signal that the public has lost patience with Za Ndani administrative defense of the status quo. There is a palpable sense that the Judiciary has been operating as a protected class, insulated from the same performance pressures that affect other sectors. People are watching closely to see if the commission will actually invite public participation or if these rules will remain locked behind the heavy doors of the Supreme Court building.

### Can Internal Discipline Suffice?

The failure of past disciplinary efforts often boiled down to a lack of clear, actionable triggers for minor infractions. In the past, judicial officers accused of lethargy or subtle bias were rarely held to account because the system focused almost exclusively on corruption cases that could be proven in a criminal court. By creating a middle ground, the commission is betting that a tiered penalty system will act as a deterrent. However, critics argue that without external oversight, the culture of "protecting our own" will continue to shield officers from the consequences of their actions.

The next phase involves extensive consultations with the National Assembly Committee on Delegated Legislation. These meetings will be the true litmus test for whether the judiciary is willing to open its internal kitchen to outside inspection. The country is waiting to see if the Gavel will finally mirror the people it serves, or if it will continue to strike only when it deems necessary.

The public mood remains one of weary expectation, where hope for genuine change is tempered by a long history of institutional stalling.
