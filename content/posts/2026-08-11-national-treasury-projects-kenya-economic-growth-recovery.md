## The Growth Mirage: Why Kenyan Business Owners Are Doubting Treasury’s 5.1% Forecast National Treasury Projects Economic Growth Recovery for Kenya is the central subject of the update.

The National Treasury has officially lowered the 2026 growth expectation to 5.0 percent, while dangling a 5.1 percent target for 2027 in its latest Budget Review and Outlook Paper released this Tuesday. For the people running businesses, these numbers feel like a different reality entirely.

The document released this August 11 outlines a path toward recovery built on the promise of private sector investment and a gradual easing of global supply chain pressures. While state planners are banking on a future where domestic production stabilizes, the Treasury admits that rising energy costs and the shadow of geopolitical tensions in the Middle East remain constant threats to this balance. The strategy hinges on the government divesting from certain state-owned enterprises to create space for private capital, a move intended to stimulate the stagnant job market and ease foreign exchange pressures.

### Disconnect Between Paper and Pavement

In the markets of Nairobi and the industrial workshops near the Rironi-Mau Summit corridor, the Treasury’s projections are met with heavy skepticism. For the local manufacturer or the owner of a small machine shop, a macro-level growth projection of 5 percent does not translate to their bottom line when the cost of electricity remains a massive monthly hurdle. Many are asking how the government intends to foster growth when the overheads of running a simple business have not relented.

"They talk about growth on paper, but my power bill tells a story of survival, not expansion," says Samuel Omondi, who manages a welding fabrication shop in Industrial Area. He notes that while the government speaks of private sector-led recovery, the reality on the ground involves choosing between paying rent and buying raw materials that have doubled in price over the last year. For him, the theoretical 5 percent growth feels like a figure designed for international lenders rather than local entrepreneurs.

### The Real Cost of Doing Business

The frustration extends to the Jua Kali sector, where artisans and metalworkers are struggling to reconcile their daily input costs with the optimistic outlook from the state. Many small business owners argue that the high cost of fuel directly negates any theoretical gains in productivity. When transport costs to move goods from the port or the farm to the city center keep climbing, profit margins simply evaporate, regardless of what the national outlook suggests.

Grace Wanjiku, who runs a furniture assembly line, points out that the talk of divestiture and public-private partnerships rarely trickles down to those needing small-scale credit to grow. Instead, she sees rising tax burdens and utility prices that make it harder to compete with cheaper, imported goods. To her, the 5.1 percent forecast is a projection that assumes the private sector has the capacity to breathe, when in reality, it is currently struggling to find enough oxygen to stay afloat.

### Looking Ahead to 2027

The Treasury remains firm that stability will return, provided that internal investment holds steady and the global climate does not deteriorate further. However, the disconnect between state planning and the micro-economy remains the defining feature of this fiscal cycle. While the government prepares to offload state entities to private investors, many small businesses are waiting for a more fundamental shift: a reduction in the costs of production that currently stifle their ability to innovate or expand.

As the government turns its attention to the 2027 cycle, the mood in the streets remains guarded. People are watching the headlines, but they are measuring the country's health by the prices of their inputs and the size of their daily sales rather than percentages on a government report. Whether this growth manifests as tangible relief or remains a statistical exercise is a question that local business owners are still asking, even as the official projections are etched into the record.

The public sentiment is one of weary skepticism, where hope for a recovery is held hostage by the immediate, crushing weight of operational costs.
