## The Grounded Flights of Sovereignty

The Kenya Aviation Workers Union walked off the job this morning, leaving thousands of passengers stranded in terminals across the country. Official statements blame the disruption on stalled salary negotiations, but the timing reveals a deeper, more calculated resistance against the systematic handover of national assets.

### The Adani Shadow Over JKIA

While the Kenya Airports Authority issues predictable advisories about flight delays and terminal gridlock, the silence regarding the proposed Adani Group lease is deafening. This is not merely a dispute over pay scales or collective bargaining agreements. The labor unrest serves as a barricade against a thirty-year deal that would effectively place Jomo Kenyatta International Airport under external private control. The government suggests that such partnerships are necessary to modernize infrastructure, yet the reality is that the aviation workforce is staring down a future where their employer is no longer accountable to the Kenyan public, but to a conglomerate headquartered in Ahmedabad.

The concern is not just about the loss of jobs but the loss of national agency. When infrastructure is leased to entities with little concern for local labor laws or long-term fiscal transparency, the state becomes a glorified landlord in its own house. This transition mimics the broader struggle documented in recent discourse, where strategic connections seem to dictate the terms of success far more than the actual labor or personal effort of those maintaining the country's most vital systems. If the JKIA deal proceeds as currently modeled, the revenue-sharing framework ensures that the most profitable aspects of airport operations are exported rather than reinvested into the Kenyan economy.

### Behind the Moses Ndiema Ultimatum

Union leadership, spearheaded by Moses Ndiema, has made it clear that the union will not be a silent partner in the erosion of public infrastructure. The strike notice, originally filed back in July, was never really about a simple salary increment. It was a formal declaration that the workforce refuses to be the collateral damage in a privatization scheme designed to bypass public scrutiny. Ndiema has repeatedly emphasized that the union will not accept terms that lead to the mass termination of existing contracts, a phenomenon that has already forced many skilled professionals into a cycle of precarious employment or outright migration.

The government maintains that current management models are inefficient, citing aging equipment and stagnant revenue growth. Yet, the paper trail suggests that the proposed private management model prioritizes high-yield extraction over long-term stability. By demanding external control over security, maintenance, and revenue collection, the deal sidelines the very people who know the operational failures of the facility better than any foreign consultant. The industrial action effectively highlights the disconnect between the rapid-fire signing of international leases and the gritty reality of workers who know exactly what happens to service quality when profit margins dictate maintenance schedules.

### The Price of Privatization

The current gridlock at the airport serves as a physical manifestation of a broken trust. When labor relations hit a dead end, it is usually because one party is attempting to rewrite the rules of the game without the consent of those tasked with running the engine. If the state chooses to prioritize a lucrative long-term contract with an external entity over the collective bargaining rights of its aviation staff, it invites a total collapse of its busiest transport hub.

Is this nation prepared to trade its strategic infrastructure for the promise of private efficiency, knowing fully that once the gates are handed over to outside stakeholders, the lever of accountability will be permanently removed from the hands of the Kenyan people?
