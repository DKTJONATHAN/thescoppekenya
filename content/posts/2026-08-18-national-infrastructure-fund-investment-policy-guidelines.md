## New Financial Benchmarks for Infrastructure and What They Mean for You National Infrastructure Fund Sets Strict Returns and Risk Rules is the central subject of the update.

The National Infrastructure Fund has adopted a clear set of financial criteria for all new development projects. This transition shifts the focus of national construction away from state-led initiatives toward a model based on commercial viability and asset divestiture. These changes are designed to influence how national resources are allocated, with potential downstream effects on trade costs and economic stability.

For most Kenyans, the planning of power grids, airports, and major roads feels like a distant process. However, the newly appointed six-member board, which includes chair James Mworia alongside members such as Fahima Ali Ahmed Zein and Christopher Kibui Maranga, has introduced a more rigorous financial screening process. By prioritizing projects that show strong indicators of financial health, the fund is effectively setting new criteria for which developments receive backing and which are set aside. When funding is tied to performance expectations, planners focus on assets that generate consistent revenue. This approach favors initiatives like toll roads or modernized airport logistics over projects that provide social utility but lack a clear path to direct profitability.

### How Funding Requirements Affect the Cost of Living

Infrastructure development acts as a foundational element of the cost of living. When the government utilizes proceeds from asset divestiture to finance upgrades, such as the improvement of the Thika-Namanga corridor or the expansion of Jomo Kenyatta International Airport, the goal is to reduce the costs associated with transport and trade. Under the current framework, if a logistics project cannot demonstrate an ability to cover its own expenses through user fees or clear efficiency gains, it is unlikely to secure funding.

This creates an environment where each investment must produce measurable results. This disciplined approach is intended to curb the inefficient spending seen in past public sector projects. At the same time, it prioritizes developments that maximize commercial throughput. For traders and commuters, this could eventually result in cheaper, more reliable transit. However, it also requires a change in how the public perceives state-funded goods. If a project, such as a rural bridge or small-scale irrigation scheme, does not meet the necessary financial criteria, those areas may see less focus compared to high-traffic urban corridors.

### Balancing Sectoral Growth and Investment Limits

The fund has established a 40 percent cap on investments within any single sector and a 20 percent limit for individual projects to maintain a diversified portfolio. This strategy is intended to mitigate risk. While this policy protects against the impact of a single project failure, it also defines the scope of available work. By requiring the fund to spread its capital, the board is limiting the scale of any single massive development.

This diversification approach is a move to protect the economy from the shocks that could arise if one large project were to falter. The result is that capital is spread across various sectors, including energy, water management, and transport. For the labor market, this suggests that the nature of available work is changing. Rather than relying on large, centralized hiring efforts for a single corridor, employment opportunities are becoming more varied and distributed across the country.

### Managing Risk Through Fiscal Discipline

A key requirement of the new mandate is that at least 60 percent of project financing must be non-recourse. This means that debt associated with these projects must be repaid through the project's own revenue rather than through the national treasury. This marks a departure from previous decades, when the state often absorbed the losses of unsuccessful construction ventures.

This financial discipline is designed to prevent infrastructure from becoming a long-term burden on taxpayers. It serves as a contrast to recent administrative challenges, such as the identified irregularities in payroll systems, which highlight the risks associated with opaque spending. By requiring projects to stand on their own financial merits, the fund is attempting to protect national assets from the political cycles that often lead to abandoned or incomplete construction. The board members serve three-year terms to maintain accountability, supported by a five-year policy horizon that allows for long-term planning. Ultimately, the new era of infrastructure is defined by a requirement that projects justify their existence through their ability to generate value, ensuring that public resources are directed toward sustainable and productive assets.
