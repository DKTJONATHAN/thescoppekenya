## Aviation Liberalization and the Sovereignty of Local Skies

The National Assembly has issued a formal call for public submissions concerning seventeen pending bilateral air services agreements, marking a shift toward aggressive market liberalization. This legislative move seeks to formalize reciprocal traffic rights with global partners, ostensibly to solidify Nairobi as a premier regional transit node.

### The Readiness Gap in Domestic Skies

Kenya Airways, currently grappling with a long-term restructuring process, faces a potential existential squeeze as these seventeen agreements threaten to flood the market with high-capacity international carriers. While the government promotes the ambition of a continental hub, the current operational reality shows that our national carrier is not yet positioned to face such an influx of subsidized foreign competition on equal footing. If the domestic fleet cannot scale its operations to match the frequencies permitted under these new pacts, local SMEs that rely on efficient air cargo logistics may find their supply chains dictated by foreign entities rather than local operators. 

The strategy appears to prioritize volume over the sustainability of our own aviation ecosystem. Analysts are questioning whether the Departmental Committee on Transport and Infrastructure has sufficiently weighed the cost of market entry for international players against the fragile recovery of Kenya Airways. Without protective safeguards or phased implementation, there is a risk that these agreements could cannibalize local market share before our domestic firms have the technological and financial leverage to compete.

### Infrastructure Constraints at JKIA

Beyond the competitive threat to local carriers, the physical reality at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport remains a significant hurdle. Increased throughput resulting from seventeen new aviation pacts demands more than just legal signatures; it requires a radical upgrade in handling capacity, apron space, and passenger processing efficiency. The airport is already nearing its operational ceiling during peak transit hours, and a surge in foreign airline frequencies could exacerbate the current bottlenecks. 

Unless these agreements are tethered to firm commitments for infrastructural investment, the policy risks creating an environment of chronic congestion. The legislative review must determine if the state has secured the capital required to expand terminal facilities to accommodate the projected surge. Policy makers are currently balancing the allure of liberalized skies against the technical limitations of our primary port of entry, a tension that has become as volatile as the market itself, much like the recent volatility seen in [Thee Pluto's Automated Trading: A High-Risk Hustle](https://zandani.co.ke/article/thee-plutos-automated-trading-high-risk-hustle).

### Legislative Oversight and Long-term Preservation

The Departmental Committee on Transport and Infrastructure occupies a precarious position as it begins the public feedback phase. Their mandate is to ensure that while international connectivity grows, the fundamental rights and operational security of the domestic aviation sector are not discarded in the name of modernization. Critics suggest that the government must incorporate clauses that require foreign airlines to invest in local ground-handling services or partner with local firms to ensure that the economic benefits of the deal remain onshore. 

This process echoes broader regional trends where trade liberalization often outpaces the capacity of developing domestic industries. The public feedback process is expected to be contentious, as stakeholders push for clauses that protect against predatory pricing and ensure equitable landing slot allocations. While the goal is to increase the footprint of our skies, the process must eventually answer for the hidden costs associated with such rapid market expansion. Policy must balance the horizon.
