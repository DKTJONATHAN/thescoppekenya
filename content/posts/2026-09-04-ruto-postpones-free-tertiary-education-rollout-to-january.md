## A Broken Promise Keeps Students Stranded Ruto Postpones Free Tertiary Education Rollout to January is the central subject of the update.

Kenyan households are waking up to a harsh reality today as the much-anticipated rollout of free tertiary education has been shelved until the start of 2027. This four-month delay leaves thousands of families trapped between expired budgets and rising costs.

President William Ruto confirmed yesterday that the government’s plan to launch free higher education has been pushed from this month to January 2027. The delay is tied to the ongoing legislative process surrounding the Tertiary Placement and Funding Bill 2026, which remains under parliamentary review. While the Ministry of Education has advised students to continue relying on the current Student-Centred Funding Model to secure their places for the upcoming semester, the shift has ignited widespread frustration across the country. For many, this is not just a legislative pause; it is a financial crisis that forces them to scramble for tuition fees they had expected the government to cover.

### Voices from the Classroom Limbo

In Nairobi, the mood is one of profound exhaustion. Mary Wanjiru, a second-year student who had spent months preparing her family for the transition to the new funding model, says the news feels like a slap in the face. Her mother, a small-scale trader, had already redirected household funds elsewhere, assuming the January semester would be the only time they needed to pay full fees. Now, they are back to square one, trying to bridge a gap that was supposed to be closed by policy reform.

Down in Kimana, Kajiado County, the sentiment is even more desperate. John Ole Kipas, a student waiting to join a technical college, describes the situation as a dangerous purgatory. He noted that the family had already sold part of their harvest to cover the initial admission costs, expecting the free education promise to kick in immediately. With the shift to January, he faces the prospect of sitting at home for four months, watching his peers move forward while he remains stuck in a cycle of uncertainty.

Meanwhile, parents are counting the cost of the administrative standstill. Many express that they cannot simply pause their lives while the government waits for legal paperwork to clear. They point out that the cost of living has already stretched their resources to the limit, and this delay removes the one safety net they were banking on to keep their children in school. The feeling on the ground is that the legislative process has moved far away from the reality of the average student.

### The Cost of Waiting

The transition between the old funding model and the proposed free tertiary program is not merely a bureaucratic checkbox. Under the existing framework, students must undergo rigorous vetting processes to qualify for support, a system that many have described as slow and often inaccessible. The promise of the new program was to simplify this path and broaden the scope of relief. By pushing the timeline, the government has effectively extended the lifespan of a system that many households have already identified as insufficient for their economic needs.

The economic strain is compounded by the fact that many families planned their entire annual budget around a September shift. January is notoriously difficult for Kenyan families, often arriving immediately after the December festive season and coinciding with high primary and secondary school demand. Moving a major financial obligation to the first month of the new year creates a double burden that many are ill-equipped to handle. While lawmakers debate the merits of the Tertiary Placement and Funding Bill, the silence from the government on immediate bridging support for the most vulnerable families grows louder.

As the country turns its attention to other matters, such as the retirement of veteran referee Anthony Taylor or the ongoing discussions around ethical artificial intelligence, the students caught in this transition remain in limbo. The government maintains that the legislation must be airtight before funds are released, but for those in Kajiado and Nairobi, the wait feels like a slow-motion cancellation of their aspirations. The public mood is firm: a policy is only as good as its timing, and right now, the timing is leaving the most vulnerable behind.
