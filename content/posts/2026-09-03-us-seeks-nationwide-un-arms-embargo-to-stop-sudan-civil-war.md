## Washington Plays Hide and Seek with the Sudan Conflict

The United States is suddenly demanding a nationwide arms embargo for Sudan, citing the involvement of 12 unnamed nations. Yet, by withholding the identities of these suppliers, Washington is effectively sanitizing its own geopolitical interests while leaving regional security-including our own-in the dark.

The declaration from Massad Boulos, acting on behalf of the Trump administration, shifts the focus from the suffering of the Sudanese people to a tactical blame game. While the humanitarian narrative captures the headlines, the strategic reality is that the US is now lobbying for a total shutdown of weapons flow, specifically targeting drone technology. This push arrives with a suspicious lack of transparency. If there are indeed 12 states fueling this fire, why does the paper trail remain buried in classified dossiers? By refusing to name these actors, the US is not providing a roadmap for peace; it is wielding a diplomatic hammer that keeps potential regional allies and adversaries alike under a permanent state of nervous compliance.

### The Fiction of the Arms Embargo

Previous attempts to police conflict zones in Africa via UN-mandated arms embargoes have been a masterclass in failure. Darfur serves as the grim blueprint for this incompetence. Despite years of restrictions, the sophisticated weaponry-and increasingly, the drone fleets-that dominate the battlefield today have bypassed every bureaucratic hurdle thrown in their path. The drone technology currently in play represents a tactical leap that traditional UN monitoring teams are fundamentally unequipped to track, let alone intercept.

The silence surrounding the identities of the 12 nations is the most telling aspect of this policy shift. Any state currently arming either the Sudanese Armed Forces or the Rapid Support Forces is, by definition, a player in the Horn of Africa's power structure. Many of these silent partners share borders or deep trade ties with Kenya. If Nairobi is to maintain its diplomatic balancing act, it cannot afford to be blindsided by a US-led sanctions list that treats regional stability as a secondary concern to American strategic posture. Our proximity to the crater of this conflict means that every crate of munitions flowing into Sudan eventually ripples across our northern borders, yet we are expected to applaud a move that prioritizes Washington’s definitions of regional order over local security realities.

### Strategic Silence and Regional Risks

The push for a nationwide ban rather than localized restrictions suggests that the US is moving away from managing the crisis toward a wholesale containment strategy. This shift is not just about the war in Sudan. It is about locking in an alliance structure that secures American influence in the Horn of Africa as a safeguard against emerging shifts in continental loyalties. By keeping the list of 12 countries secret, the US ensures it can choose when to use that information as leverage and when to bury it in the name of diplomatic necessity.

This approach mirrors the opacity we see in domestic governance, where accountability is often sacrificed for the convenience of the powerful. Just as the public remains frustrated by the lack of transparency in the handling of public infrastructure, as seen in the recent controversies over road projects in Homa Bay, the international public is being asked to trust a process that lacks visible accountability. The death of figures like Félicien Kabuga serves as a reminder that justice often moves at a glacial pace, and by the time it arrives, the underlying systemic issues have already mutated into new crises.

As the UN considers this broader embargo, the question remains whether this is a genuine intervention or merely a new layer of geopolitical shielding. If the intent is truly to choke off the supply lines that are tearing a neighbor apart, why protect the very architects of that instability by keeping their names off the record? Who exactly is being shielded by this diplomatic shadow play, and at what cost to the stability of the entire region?
