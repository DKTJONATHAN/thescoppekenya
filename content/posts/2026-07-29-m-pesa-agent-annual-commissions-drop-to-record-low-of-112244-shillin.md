## The decline of the mobile money agent

### The paradox of digital convenience
The widespread adoption of mobile money has fundamentally transformed the Kenyan economy, making digital payments standard for even the smallest purchases like roasted maize. However, this total integration has created an ironic crisis for the agents who facilitate these transactions. As the system becomes more seamless and ubiquitous, the necessity for the traditional agent model has diminished. The saturation of the market, once a sign of success, has now effectively cannibalized the income potential for those operating physical booths.

### Rising competition and operational costs
The era of a single dominant platform is over. Mobile money agents now face significant pressure from rival services including Airtel Money and a growing network of bank-affiliated agency services. This increased competition has fractured the market share once held exclusively by M-Pesa. Consequently, agents are trapped between rising operational overheads and diminishing profit margins. While transaction volumes remain high, the individual commissions earned per transaction have plummeted, leaving many operators unable to sustain their small businesses.

### The shift from storefront to cloud
The financial landscape of mobile transactions has shifted, moving the bulk of profits away from ground-level agents and toward digital infrastructure. While the platforms themselves thrive on high-frequency digital traffic, the individuals stationed in kiosks are struggling to reconcile their high costs with their shrinking returns. This economic reality is forcing many long-term operators to reconsider the viability of their business model. For many, the financial rewards of the industry no longer justify the labor, leading to a steady decline in the number of active agents operating on the streets.
