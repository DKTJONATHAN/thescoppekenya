## The Absa Insurance Shell Game Absa Group to Exit Direct Insurance Underwriting in Kenya is the central subject of the update.

Absa Group is offloading a 63.32 percent stake in First Assurance and Absa Life Assurance Kenya for 2.9 billion KSh. By retreating from the high-risk mandate of underwriting, the banking giant is cleverly extracting its capital while tightening its grip on lucrative, low-risk fee income.

The narrative from the boardroom suggests a tidy portfolio refinement, but the balance sheet tells a story of tactical abandonment. Underwriting insurance is a capital-heavy burden requiring rigorous solvency margins and a constant exposure to the volatility of claims. By selling these subsidiaries, Absa effectively scrubs its books of those long-term liabilities. Yet, the bank is not walking away from the insurance sector. It is merely pivoting to the part of the business that requires no capital, no reserve assets, and no vulnerability to the rising tide of local insurance payouts: the distribution channel.

### The Profitability of the Middleman

Bancassurance is the golden goose for modern lenders. It allows Absa Bank Kenya to leverage its massive customer base to sell policies while someone else carries the risk. Under the new arrangement, the bank retains its ironclad hold on the distribution network. They remain the gatekeeper, funnelling premiums from unsuspecting account holders to whichever entity ultimately replaces them as the underwriter. 

This shift mirrors the broader trend seen across Botswana, Mozambique, and Zambia throughout 2025. The bank is systematically shedding the "insurance" part of the business while doubling down on the "brokerage" part. For the bank, the return on equity for distributing policies is vastly superior to the returns generated by maintaining the statutory capital requirements demanded by the Insurance Regulatory Authority. While independent firms struggle to keep up with the ballooning capital adequacy requirements in a fluctuating market, Absa is choosing to exit the arena entirely, leaving behind the regulatory heat while keeping the cold, hard cash flowing from commissions.

### Who Carries the Bag?

The 2.9 billion KSh transaction-comprising 2.2 billion KSh for First Assurance and 700 million KSh for the life business-is a calculated move to prune assets that were clearly not delivering the required growth. However, this raises a darker question about the quality of the insurance products being hawked at the teller counter. When a bank controls the sales channel but loses the obligation to back the policy, the incentive structure tilts sharply in favor of volume over value. 

Local policyholders often opt for bank-backed insurance under the assumption that the institution’s massive balance sheet is their guarantee. By separating the underwriting risk from the banking brand, that implicit safety net is being quietly removed. Customers are essentially being handed off to whoever the new owners are, often without the deep corporate synergy that once existed under the Absa banner. As the industry grapples with 19.51 percent concentration risks and the perpetual search for liquidity, smaller, independent firms will likely be the ones to absorb the liabilities Absa has deemed too heavy.

If the goal of the bank is to minimize exposure while maximizing fee-based revenue, what happens to the customer when the claims process hits a snag with an underwriter that lacks the bank's massive infrastructure? Is this truly a strategic reorganization, or are we witnessing the professionalized offloading of risk onto the local market while the bank remains the only one guaranteed a cut of every shilling paid in premiums?
