## Customs Logjam by Design

The Kenya Revenue Authority has ordered a complete operational blackout of the Integrated Customs Management System for twelve hours this Sunday. While tax authorities frame this as routine maintenance, the move effectively freezes the nation's primary economic artery during a period of high volume.

### The Math of a Stalled Port

At the Port of Mombasa, time is not just money; it is a metric tonne calculation. A twelve-hour suspension of all import and export declaration services creates a bottleneck that does not simply evaporate when the servers blink back to life. Conservative industry estimates suggest that a full work stoppage at the facility puts roughly 124,500 metric tonnes of cargo at immediate risk of clearance delays. This is not merely an administrative hiccup. It is an artificial blockage imposed on a supply chain already struggling to maintain momentum amidst a volatile economy.

Logistics stakeholders argue that scheduling such a significant digital blackout on a Sunday-a day often used to clear the backlog of the preceding work week-is tone-deaf to the realities of global trade. Cargo vessels do not pause their schedules because a server room in Nairobi requires an update. Every hour of downtime forces transporters to pay for yard storage, dwell time, and terminal handling charges that ultimately inflate the final cost of goods for the end consumer. While the state characterizes this as a technical necessity, it ignores the fiscal hemorrhage inflicted on clearing agents who are held accountable for delivery timelines they no longer control.

### Compliance Over Efficiency

The timing of this maintenance is particularly curious when viewed alongside the aggressive rollout of new stock management features for eTIMS. By forcing an update that mandates deeper integration of tax invoicing tools during a period of zero productivity, the KRA is signaling a shift in priorities. The agency is moving away from its role as a trade facilitator and toward an intrusive monitor of private inventory levels.

This forced evolution into inventory management shifts the burden of tax compliance directly onto the shoulders of small-scale importers. They are now tasked with aligning their internal logistics with state-mandated digital tracking, regardless of whether their business systems are prepared for such oversight. It is a classic move from a state machinery that prioritizes revenue surveillance over operational throughput. The KRA effectively treats the supply chain as a captive audience for its software testing, indifferent to the fact that the cost of these digital compliance hurdles is inevitably passed down to the taxpayer.

The focus on digital security is a recurring theme in the current administrative push, mirroring recent government efforts to tighten financial protocols, such as the measures taken to combat SIM swap fraud. However, when security and tracking upgrades come at the cost of national port functionality, the line between robust regulation and obstruction becomes dangerously thin.

The integration of stock management functionality for TIMS users is the true objective of this weekend blackout. The customs maintenance is simply the delivery vehicle for a broader surveillance apparatus. The question remains why a government purportedly committed to economic growth would choose to throttle its most vital port infrastructure to install a system designed to audit the very traders it is meant to support. If the state views importers as potential tax gaps rather than economic partners, how long can the supply chain survive this relentless focus on digital monitoring at the expense of its own mechanical efficiency?
