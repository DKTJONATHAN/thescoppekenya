## A Dangerous Mix of Gas and Politics

Energy Cabinet Secretary Opiyo Wandayi is doubling down on the ongoing crackdown against illegal liquefied petroleum gas traders, dismissing claims of state-sponsored harassment while signaling that regulatory pressure on the sector will not ease despite rising political friction from former leaders.

The government maintains that the current enforcement sweep is a critical safety intervention intended to root out unlicensed refilling plants that pose lethal risks to residential areas. This follows vocal accusations from former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua, who claimed that an unauthorized police unit has been unfairly targeting small-scale gas vendors. While the Ministry of Energy insists these operations are guided by Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) mandates to prevent domestic gas explosions, traders in counties like Kiambu and Kajiado argue the line between legitimate safety enforcement and heavy-handed harassment has become dangerously blurred.

### Voices from the Frontline

The apprehension on the ground is palpable among small-scale business owners who feel caught between official regulation and fear of the unknown. In Kajiado, local shopkeeper John Mutua says he operates with a valid permit but still keeps his doors locked whenever a white pick-up truck passes by. He explains that while he supports safety, the constant threat of having his stock confiscated without a clear inspection process makes it impossible to distinguish between genuine EPRA officials and the alleged shadow units being discussed in national discourse.

From Kiambu, distributor Mary Wanjiru notes that the distinction between a licensed micro-dealer and an illegal refilling syndicate is often lost during these enforcement raids. She describes a climate where even those who have paid their dues to the regulator are treated with immediate suspicion. According to Wanjiru, the anxiety stems from a lack of transparency in how these raids are conducted, leaving many small traders wondering if their livelihoods are being sacrificed for a political optics game rather than genuine public safety.

Technical experts who track energy compliance suggest the confusion lies in the methodology. While legitimate EPRA enforcement typically involves a structured audit of pressure test logs and certification of cylinders, the alleged clandestine units often bypass these protocols. Industry observers point out that true safety enforcement should be educational and systematic, focusing on the illegal decanting plants that operate in backyards and industrial sheds, rather than targeting the small-scale retail kiosks that act as the final link in the supply chain for low-income households.

### Navigating Regulatory Reality

The regulatory landscape is becoming increasingly difficult to decipher. As the Treasury recently initiated an early budget cycle and tightened controls on public spending, the pressure on agencies like EPRA to show results has intensified. This creates an environment where aggressive enforcement serves both a safety purpose and a political one. For the consumer, the main concern remains the affordability of cooking energy, which often fluctuates when the informal supply chain is disrupted by state actions.

Despite these tensions, the government remains firm on its objective to reduce the estimated 200,000 to 500,000 unsafe cylinders that experts believe are currently circulating in Kenyan homes. The focus moving forward will likely be on whether the Ministry of Energy can establish a standardized inspection protocol that separates compliant small-scale dealers from the dangerous syndicates. Until a clear vetting process is communicated to the public, the tension between vendors and the state is expected to persist.

Public anxiety remains high as the government prepares for the next phase of its regulatory enforcement, leaving many wondering if the safety of the kitchen will take precedence over the politics of the street.
