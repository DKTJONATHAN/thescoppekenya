## The 1965 Blueprint vs. Modern Road Reality

Homa Bay County residents recently vented their frustrations over crumbling road networks during the Linda Mwananchi rally held on August 16. The ruling UDA party responded by citing a 1965 policy as the foundational cause of this persistent regional infrastructure deficit.

### A Legacy of Selective Growth

The narrative of regional marginalization in Kenya is inextricably linked to Sessional Paper No. 10 of 1965. This landmark document advocated for concentrating government investment in areas already demonstrating high economic potential. The logic was simple yet exclusionary: resources were steered toward regions with established agricultural and industrial output, effectively starving areas like South Nyanza of the capital required for connectivity.

For decades, this economic framework provided a convenient justification for the lack of road networks in Western Kenya. While the 2010 Constitution was designed specifically to dismantle this top-down investment model through the introduction of devolved units, the ghost of 1965 remains a potent rhetorical tool. Nathaniel Mong'are, the UDA Communications Director, recently pivoted to this historical justification when questioned about the current state of infrastructure in Homa Bay, suggesting that the party is merely inheriting a structural failure that predates the modern political establishment.

### Devolution Under the Microscope

The 2010 Constitution shifted the responsibility for local development to county governments, mandating that resources follow the people rather than the economic potential of the soil. Since 2013, over 13 years of budgetary allocations have flowed into the devolved units, intended to bypass the rigid, colonial-era investment models of the past.

Governor Gladys Wanga has consistently argued that the infrastructure gaps in Homa Bay are the cumulative result of historical political activism and the subsequent punitive neglect by central governments. Yet, fiscal records show a more complex reality. While historical grievances are grounded in truth, the current county administration has substantial autonomy over local development priorities. Critics suggest that blaming a document from the mid-sixties is a calculated move to deflect from the granular challenges of managing regional road maintenance and local contractor oversight. The debate effectively masks whether current funding is being utilized to modernize infrastructure or if the county is becoming a theater for historical blame-shifting.

### The Fiscal Disconnect

*   1965: Sessional Paper No. 10 establishes the "growth pole" theory, prioritizing regions with immediate economic returns and formalizing decades of neglect for peripheral zones.
*   2013: The commencement of the devolved government system creates a new fiscal reality, meant to decentralize road development and empower local authorities to address regional needs independently.
*   2022: Post-election, the gap between national government infrastructure promises and the actual delivery of road projects in Homa Bay reaches a critical friction point, with 22,000 kilometres of road works highlighted as a national priority.

This administrative deadlock leaves the average resident caught between two narratives. The UDA leadership points backward to a colonial-era logic to rationalize current inaction, while county leaders point to a history of political isolation to explain persistent delays. This creates a convenient cycle where fiscal accountability is lost in the fog of historical grievances. As seen in other contexts, such as the [low voter turnout in Ol Kalou UDA primaries](https://zandani.co.ke/article/low-voter-turnout-in-ol-kalou-uda-primaries-raises-concerns-after-ca), citizens are increasingly weary of political rhetoric that prioritizes historical context over tangible utility.

The infrastructure debate in Homa Bay suggests that when political actors cannot bridge the gap between promises and pavement, they simply reach further back into the archives to find an alibi. History rarely clears the road.
