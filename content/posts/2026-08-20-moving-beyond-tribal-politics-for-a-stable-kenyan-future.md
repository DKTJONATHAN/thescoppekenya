## The Tribalism Paradox: Why Politicians Are Actually Terrified of Policy-Based Politics

The prevailing wisdom in Kenyan political circles suggests that tribalism is an ancient, incurable cultural ailment that politicians are simply forced to navigate to win elections. This is a comforting lie that excuses systemic mediocrity. In reality, tribalism is the primary shield used by the inept to hide their absolute inability to govern.

As the 2027 electoral cycle approaches, the political theatre is already descending into the familiar trenches of communal loyalty. We are told the voter demands this, but it is the political class that provides it. By reducing the national discourse to ethnic arithmetic, candidates avoid the grueling task of articulating complex economic strategies or healthcare reforms. If a politician can secure a bloc by merely whispering to their base, they have no incentive to study a budget, propose a tax policy, or understand the intricacies of foreign trade. Tribalism is not a reflection of our culture. It is a strategic mechanism for shielding hollow candidates from having to pass a basic competency test.

### The Economic Cost of Empty Rhetoric

The financial toll of these policy-free campaigns is staggering. Every year lost to ethno-political maneuvering is a year where structural economic development stagnates. We treat elections as a zero-sum game of identity, ignoring that the market does not care which community holds the levers of power if those leaders cannot manage a national balance sheet. When candidates run on communal pride rather than developmental track records, they effectively sell the electorate a dream of prestige while delivering a reality of debt and stalled infrastructure. This is the ultimate cynical trade-off: a candidate offers a seat at the table of power in exchange for the voter’s silence on why the cost of living remains unmanageable.

Consider Za Ndanik contrast between two archetypes of leadership. Nelson Mandela prioritized a unified vision that transcended racial and tribal lines to build a stable institutional framework, focusing on the heavy lifting of constitutional reconciliation. On the other end of the spectrum, figures like Adolf Hitler utilized populist division to bypass complex societal issues, weaponizing grievances to consolidate power through fear and tribal scapegoating. Kenyan politicians who rely on inflammatory rhetoric are borrowing from the latter playbook, not because they are inherently malicious, but because it is the path of least resistance for a mediocre mind. It is significantly easier to incite an emotional response based on identity than it is to draft a comprehensive strategy for agricultural output or industrial growth.

### The Illusion of Voter Demand

Politicians frequently claim they are merely reflecting the will of the people, yet they are the ones funding the divisions. If the voter were truly the driver, we would see a surge in independent, policy-focused candidates gaining traction. Instead, we see established power brokers moving between parties that share no distinct philosophy, merely rearranging their alliances to capture specific ethnic pockets. It is a cynical rotation that keeps the status quo intact. Whether the conversation turns to the aftermath of a high-profile shooting investigation like the PS Fikirini Jacobs case or the superficial distraction of a headline-grabbing concert like the Kirk Whalum and Alikiba event, the public sphere is constantly bombarded with noise that pushes substantive policy issues to the margins.

True political evolution requires that we stop viewing elections as identity contests and start treating them as hiring processes for management. We should look for candidates who provide specific, measurable deliverables. If a candidate cannot explain how they plan to lower the cost of production for small businesses without mentioning their community, they should not be on the ballot. Rejecting the tribal shield is the only way to expose the incompetence currently masquerading as leadership. The time has come to stop blaming the electorate for the deficiencies of those who are paid to serve them.
