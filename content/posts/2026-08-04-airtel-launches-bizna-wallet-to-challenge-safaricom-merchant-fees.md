## The New Arithmetic of the Kenyan Informal Economy Airtel Launches Bizna Wallet to Challenge Safaricom Merchant Fees is the central subject of the update.

Airtel Kenya has rolled out the Bizna Wallet to aggressively court the nation’s 2.1 million micro-merchants, signaling an end to the era of passive market dominance. This shift signals a calculated move to capture liquidity within the informal sector by weaponizing transaction costs against the established incumbent.

The launch of the Bizna Wallet is not merely a product expansion; it is a direct challenge to the fiscal gravity Safaricom has held over small traders for years. By introducing a 50 percent cashback offer on transactions starting from KES 101, Airtel is effectively subsidizing the digital migration of kiosk owners, street vendors, and market stall operators. This pricing strategy attempts to undercut the traditional fee structures that have long defined the mobile money sector.

### Evaluating the Cost of Loyalty

The current competition between the two telecommunications giants highlights a dramatic inflation in customer acquisition costs. Safaricom has responded to the pressure by adjusting transaction thresholds, specifically maintaining a KES 200 free transaction limit for its Pochi La Biashara service. While the incumbent relies on an entrenched ecosystem and high barrier-to-exit, Airtel is betting that liquid cash incentives will override this convenience.

For the micro-merchant, the math is becoming increasingly complex. Between Airtel’s cashback incentives and Safaricom’s capped transfer fees of KES 50, small business owners are finding themselves in a rare position of leverage. However, this liquidity war carries a long-term risk. These platform subsidies are temporary, with the current promotional period set to conclude on October 31, 2026. The question remains whether these businesses will remain loyal once the artificial price floors are removed and organic transaction fees resume their standard trajectory.

### The Fiscal Trap of Subsidized Growth

This battle for the informal sector underscores the thin margins inherent in Kenyan mobile money. To secure market share, firms are currently trading short-term profitability for transaction volume, hoping that merchant adoption will lead to higher downstream revenue from credit and insurance products. For the average entrepreneur, the current volatility in fee structures reflects the reality that technological adoption often follows wherever the capital flows, rather than where the service is most stable.

While these shifts offer immediate relief for small businesses, they also expose the fragility of digital financial inclusion when it is dependent on corporate price wars. Reliance on these platforms is growing, yet the underlying economics of mobile money in East Africa are reaching a point of saturation. As the industry approaches the end of the year, the focus will shift from attracting new sign-ups to retaining them through actual utility rather than promotional handouts.

### Sustaining the Merchant Shift

The October 31 deadline serves as a pivot point for the entire ecosystem. If the user base remains tethered to the platforms despite the inevitable rollback of cashback offers, the strategy will be deemed a success in terms of behavioral modification. If merchants oscillate back to established services the moment costs rise, it will prove that the current competition has failed to drive fundamental innovation beyond simple fee manipulation.

Success in this market now depends on who can offer a more robust digital financial suite rather than who can offer the cheapest transaction. Investors and observers should monitor whether the increase in merchant headcount reported in March 2026 converts into permanent active daily usage or if it represents a transient wave of opportunists chasing temporary rebates.

The race to control the informal sector's wallet will ultimately be won by infrastructure, not discounts.
