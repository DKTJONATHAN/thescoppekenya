## High Court halts lawyer boycotts to restore court access

Justice David Mburu has issued conservatory orders that legally compel the Law Society of Kenya to resume participation in judicial proceedings. This intervention follows a formal petition from lawyer Adrian Kamotho, effectively pausing a standoff that has paralyzed courtrooms across the country.

### Why is your court date stuck in limbo?

For the average Kenyan, the court system is not a place for institutional power struggles; it is where property disputes, family matters, and criminal cases are resolved. When the Law Society of Kenya (LSK) directs its members to boycott specific judges or court sessions, the administrative gears of justice grind to a halt. These boycotts create a cascading effect of delays. A case that was scheduled for mention this week is pushed back, forcing litigants to pay for repeated transport to court and additional legal fees. Every hour a lawyer spends away from the courtroom during a protest is an hour added to the already massive backlog of cases waiting for a final ruling.

### How does the High Court define the limit of professional protest?

The legal friction centers on the balance between professional advocacy and the constitutional right of every Kenyan to access justice. While the LSK represents the interests of advocates, the courts serve the public at large. Justice Mburu’s ruling clarifies that professional grievances cannot supersede the functional duty of the judiciary to hear and determine cases. The court has effectively ruled that while the LSK has the right to express discontent, it cannot weaponize its presence to shut down judicial functions. This provides a necessary buffer for judges, including those previously targeted by the boycott, allowing them to proceed with their dockets without the threat of a professional strike undermining their authority.

### What is the significance of the September 17 deadline?

The court has set September 17 as the date for a follow-up hearing to further evaluate the arguments presented by both the petitioners and the LSK. This window of time is critical. It serves as a cooling-off period intended to prevent further systemic erosion of public trust in the legal process. If the stalemate persists beyond this date, the court is expected to provide deeper clarity on whether such boycotts can ever be considered a constitutional form of protest. Until then, the judiciary is legally protected from being rendered dysfunctional by the very body meant to facilitate the pursuit of law.

The human cost of this dispute is measured in the frustration of citizens whose cases remain frozen. Many who walked into courtrooms expecting a verdict or a hearing have been met with empty chairs or formal adjournments. While high-level legal strategies capture the headlines, the reality for a person awaiting a ruling on a land matter or a child custody dispute is a painful continuation of uncertainty. The order serves as a reminder that the courtroom belongs to the litigant, not the representative.

For stakeholders within the judicial system, the upcoming weeks will be a test of whether internal mechanisms can resolve disagreements without resorting to the suspension of service.
